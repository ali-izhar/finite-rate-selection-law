\begin{abstract}
Annealed Gibbs sampling can return plausible samples in the wrong proportions.
We study the Curie--Weiss model, where a weak bias, the field, favors one of
two modes. Each run selects its mode as the modes appear, when the noise of
the updates can outweigh the field, and rarely switches once they have separated. The samples
therefore keep only a fraction $c$ of the model's log-odds between the modes.
We turn the classical selection probability of Kondepudi and Nelson into a
law for $c$ that is within $2\%$ of an exact calculation on nine linear
cooling ramps. On such a ramp and for a weak field, cooling sixteen times
more slowly only doubles $c$. For a Hopfield model, assuming independent and identically distributed
pattern amplitudes, we derive a factor by which competition among patterns
raises $c$ for a pattern against its negative. On one linear ramp, point
estimates pooled over random pattern sets were within $3\%$ of this
prediction for three patterns and $6\%$ below it for four. Single pattern
sets, analyzed after the runs, departed from it beyond sampling error. The
factor thus describes at most an average over pattern sets, and the test does
not support its derivation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Suppose a model gives one mode four times the probability of another. A
sampler that returns the two modes equally often still produces likely
samples, but Monte Carlo estimates from them are biased. Annealed Gibbs
sampling, which redraws one variable at a time while the temperature falls,
can make this error even when the model is exact, because the temperature
falls at a finite rate and the sampler does not stay in equilibrium. At high temperature the model's
distribution is broad. At low temperature its probability concentrates in
modes, and a run rarely leaves its mode \citep{levinluczak2010,dlpcmp2009},
so the proportions of the samples are decided as the modes appear.

We ask what fraction $c$ of the model's log-odds between two modes the
samples keep. With $c=0.2$, these $4{:}1$ odds become
$4^{0.2}{:}1\approx1.32{:}1$. We answer for the Curie--Weiss (CW) model,
where the distribution of the samples can be computed exactly. Its binary
variables tend to agree, which gives two modes, and a weak bias, the
\emph{field}, favors one. Each run selects its mode as the
modes appear (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}a), when the noise of
the updates can outweigh the field (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}b).

We turn the classical probability of this selection, due to
\citet{kondepudinelson1983} \citep[see also][]{kondepudigao1987,nene2018},
into a law for $c$. The law explains why slower cooling helps little: on a
linear ramp and for a weak field, $c$ grows only as the fourth root of the
ramp length (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}c). For a Hopfield model, whose modes are stored
patterns and their negatives, we derive a factor by which competition raises
$c$ for a pattern against its negative. On one linear ramp, the prediction was within
$3$--$6\%$ of the $c$ pooled over random pattern sets, but single sets
departed from it (post hoc), so the factor describes at most an average over
sets and its derivation is not supported (Section~\ref{sec:test}).

\section{Model, sampler and the fraction \texorpdfstring{$c$}{c}}
\label{sec:model}

In the CW model, a \emph{configuration} is a vector $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$ of
$N$ binary variables $x_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, called spins, and configurations
whose spins agree are more probable:
\begin{equation}
 \pi_{\beta,G}(x)\propto
 \exp\!\left[\frac{\beta}{2(N-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}x_i x_j
              +\beta G\sum_i x_i\right].
 \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
The \emph{inverse temperature} $\beta$ sets how strongly spins agree, so
raising $\beta$ is cooling, and a field $G>0$ favors positive spins. At fixed
$\beta$, Gibbs sampling converges to the \emph{equilibrium distribution}
$\pi_{\beta,G}$. The \emph{magnetization} is $m=N^{-1}\sum_i x_i$. Without a field, as $N\to\infty$, the
equilibrium $m$ concentrates at $0$ for $\beta<1$ and at two opposite values
for $\beta>1$. Configurations with $m$ near either value form the two modes,
which appear at the \emph{transition}, $\beta=1$.

Each Gibbs update redraws one uniformly chosen spin. Time is measured in \emph{expected sweeps} of $N$ updates. A run
starts in equilibrium at some $\beta<1$, a cooling schedule raises $\beta$
past the transition, and the final configuration is the run's sample. Configurations with
the same number $k$ of positive spins are equally probable in equilibrium and
change $k$ at the same rates, so $k$ alone describes the run
\citep{levinluczak2010}. The \emph{exact chain} evolves its $N+1$
probabilities, instead of $2^N$, and gives the distribution of the samples
without sampling error (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).

Let $\qchain$ be the probability that a sample has $m>0$, and $\qeq$ the
probability of $m>0$ in equilibrium at the final $\beta$, both conditioned on
$m\ne0$. The model's log-odds between the modes are the equilibrium log-odds,
$\logit\qeq$, and for $\qeq\ne1/2$ the samples keep the fraction
\begin{equation}
 c=\frac{\logit\qchain}{\logit\qeq},
 \qquad \logit q=\log\frac{q}{1-q}.
 \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
A value $c=1$ reproduces the equilibrium proportions, and $c=0$ gives equal
mode probabilities.

\section{The selection law}
\label{sec:law}

Near $\beta=1$, a small random imbalance between positive and negative spins
grows into one mode (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}a), and runs rarely switch once the
modes have separated. The field can therefore bias the final proportions mainly
in a short window around the transition. The \emph{drift}, the mean rate of
change of $m$, is approximately $(\beta-1)m+G$ near $m=0$ and $\beta=1$
(Appendix~\ref{app:exact}). The first term pulls an imbalance toward zero below $\beta=1$ and
amplifies it above. On a linear ramp, $\beta(t)=1+\gamma t$ with cooling rate
$\gamma>0$, the transition is at $t=0$, and this term alone makes an
imbalance proportional to the growth factor $e^{\gamma t^2/2}$. The factor
stays near one only for
$|t|$ below a time $T$ of order $\gamma^{-1/2}$, and this interval is the
\emph{selection window}. The \emph{noise} of the updates adds a variance of
about $2/N$ per expected sweep. In the window, the field therefore adds an
imbalance of order $GT$ and the noise one of order $\sqrt{T/N}$ \citep[noise
scale:][]{berglundgentz2002}. Their ratio, of order $G\sqrt N\,\gamma^{-1/4}$,
decides whether the field or the noise sets the sign of $m$. Slower cooling
favors the field only as $\gamma^{-1/4}$.

To turn this ratio into a probability, we divide $m$ by its growth factor.
The quotient has the sign of $m$ and stops changing once the window closes,
because what the field and the noise add later is divided by a large growth
factor. Its final value is the \emph{early amplitude}. With the noise treated
as Gaussian and the drift as linear, the early amplitude is Gaussian across
runs, and in units of its standard deviation it is $U\sim\mathcal N(a,1)$,
where $a$ is the field-to-noise ratio with its exact prefactor. A run ends
with $m>0$ when $U>0$, which gives the classical selection probability
\citep{kondepudinelson1983,kondepudigao1987} (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}):
\begin{equation}
 q_{\mathrm{sel}}\simeq\Phi(a),\qquad a=G\sqrt N(\pi/\gamma)^{1/4},
 \label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
where $q_{\mathrm{sel}}$ is the probability of selecting $m>0$ and $\Phi$
the standard Gaussian CDF. In Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}b, $a=0.208$. The noise
is about five times stronger than the field, and $58\%$ of runs select the
positive mode, to which the model gives probability $0.973$.

Without later switching, $\qchain=q_{\mathrm{sel}}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:c}
then gives the \emph{selection law},
$c_{\mathrm{sel}}=\logit\Phi(a)/\logit\qeq$. For a weak field ($|a|\ll1$),
the sampled log-odds are proportional to $a$, hence to
$G\sqrt N\,\gamma^{-1/4}$. The equilibrium log-odds are proportional to $NG$,
because reversing all spins exchanges the modes and changes only the
field term $\beta GNm$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}. The field thus cancels in $c$:
\begin{equation}
 c\propto(N^2\gamma)^{-1/4},
 \label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
with a prefactor set by the final $\beta$ (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}). In this
regime $c$ does not depend on the field. Cooling sixteen times more slowly
doubles it, and a fourfold larger $N$ halves it.

The law assumes a slow ramp ($\gamma\ll1$) that starts well below $\beta=1$,
a nearly linear drift during selection, and little switching once the modes separate. Without
a field, $N\gamma\gg1$ keeps paths close to the linear dynamics until the
window closes \citep{berglundgentz2002}. Very slow cooling restores
equilibrium by switching, but at $N=2048$ and $\beta=1.1$ a zero-field switch takes
about $5\times10^{7}$ sweeps ($5\times10^{4}$ at $N=1024$; Appendix~\ref{app:exact}), and our
ramps last at most $3600$. On the slowest ramp at $N=1024$, $12\%$ of runs still change the sign of $m$ after $\beta=1+3\sqrt\gamma$, but the law matches the final $c$ to $1\%$.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Exact check.}\hspace{1em}%
On nine linear ramps ($\beta=0.7$ to $1.6$, $N=1024$--$4096$,
$\gamma=0.00025$--$0.004$, $NG=1.2544$), the exact $c$
exceeded $c_{\mathrm{sel}}$, with $a$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}, by
$0.23$--$1.81\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}c; Table~\ref{tab:linear} in
Appendix~\ref{app:exact}), although $N\gamma$ is as low as $0.26$ (range $0.26$--$16$). We did not derive the excess.

\section{Competition among several modes}
\label{sec:test}

To ask how competition among several modes changes $c$, we use the Hopfield
model, whose modes at the temperatures we use are $p\ll N$ stored
patterns $\xi^\mu\in\{-1,+1\}^N$ and their negatives \citep{ags1985}. In
Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}, the coupling $x_ix_j$ becomes
$\sum_\mu\xi_i^\mu\xi_j^\mu x_ix_j$ and the field term becomes
$\beta G\sum_i\xi_i^1x_i$. The pattern entries are independent and
equiprobable, and a random draw of the $p$ patterns is a \emph{pattern set}.
The \emph{overlap} $m^\mu=N^{-1}\sum_i\xi_i^\mu x_i$ is $+1$ at pattern $\mu$
and $-1$ at its negative, and pattern $\mu$ \emph{wins} a run when $|m^\mu|$
is largest in the run's sample. The field favors pattern~1 over its negative,
so we define $c$ for this pair: among the runs that pattern~1 wins, $\qchain$
is the probability that $m^1>0$, and $\qeq$ is its equilibrium counterpart. This $c$ does not measure how often each pattern wins.

\paragraph{Prediction.}
We assume that the early amplitudes of the $p$ overlaps are independent
Gaussians of common variance, that
the field shifts only the mean of pattern~1's amplitude, and that the pattern
whose amplitude is largest in magnitude wins. If pattern~1's
amplitude has magnitude $r$, the log-odds that it is positive, its \emph{sign
log-odds}, are the log ratio of a shifted Gaussian's densities at $+r$
and $-r$, which is proportional to $r$. Winning depends only on the magnitudes, so it leaves
these sign log-odds unchanged at each $r$, but the winner among $p$
amplitudes has a larger average magnitude than a single amplitude
(Figure~\ref{fig:competition}a). Under these assumptions and to first order
in the field, competition multiplies pattern~1's sign log-odds by the ratio
of the two averages (Appendix~\ref{app:amplification}):
\begin{equation}
 A_p=\frac{\mathbb E\max_{1\le\mu\le p}|Z_\mu|}{\mathbb E|Z|},
 \qquad c_{\mathrm{pred}}=A_p c_{\mathrm{CW}},
 \label{eq:amplification}
\end{equation}
where $Z$ and the $Z_\mu$ are independent standard Gaussians, so
$A_2=\sqrt2$, $A_3=1.662$ and $A_4=1.836$. Here $c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ is the
exact-chain fraction of CW at the same $N$, field and ramp, and
$c_{\mathrm{pred}}$ assumes that the other patterns change neither
pattern~1's field-to-noise ratio nor the equilibrium log-odds.

\paragraph{Test.}
We simulated Gibbs runs on the fastest ramp of the exact check
($\gamma=0.004$), where $N\gamma$ is largest, with $p=3$ at $N=1024$, $2048$ and $4096$ and $p=4$ at $N=2048$ ($50$
pattern sets per case, $10{,}800$ to $29{,}200$ runs per set). The measured $c$ is the log-odds that $m^1$ has the field's
sign among the runs that pattern~1 won, pooled over the sets, divided by the
exact CW value of $\logit\qeq$. Runs with $p\le2$ are controls with exact predictions
(Appendix~\ref{app:amplification}).

\paragraph{Result.}
The controls reproduced their exact predictions (ratios $0.990$--$1.015$;
Figure~\ref{fig:competition}b; Table~\ref{tab:transfer} in
Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). For three patterns,
the pooled $c$ was within $3\%$ of $A_3c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ at all three sizes,
with $95\%$ intervals that contain the prediction but allow deficits of up to
$6\%$. This $c$ fell as $N^{-0.50}$ (exponent interval $[0.47,0.53]$), as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling} predicts at a fixed rate. For four patterns, the
pooled $c$ was $6\%$ below $A_4c_{\mathrm{CW}}$, at the edge of significance
(ratio interval $[0.884,1.00]$).

Single pattern sets departed from the prediction in a post hoc analysis
(Figure~\ref{fig:competition}c; Table~\ref{tab:sets}).
Equation~\eqref{eq:amplification} treats the patterns alike, so in every set
pattern~1 should win a share $1/p$ of the runs. For four patterns, its share
ranged from $0.09$ to $0.71$. For $p\ge3$, the $c$ of a set fell as this
share rose and, beyond sampling error, varied between sets with a standard
deviation of $7$--$13\%$ of the prediction. The mean over sets of measured
over predicted $c$ is nevertheless $0.99$--$1.01$; the pooled $c$ is lower
because it weights each set by the runs that pattern~1 won.

\paragraph{Reading.}
The factor is thus close to the equally weighted mean over sets (post hoc)
but not to a single set. The assumption that the patterns of a set are alike
fails, and the test does not support the derivation. Of the two protocol
readings that apply, the less favorable gives the same verdict
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\section{Limitations and related work}
\label{sec:limits}

Why single pattern sets differ is open. One candidate cause is the chance
correlation between independent random patterns, of order $N^{-1/2}$, which
makes the variances of the amplitudes unequal unless $p/(N\gamma)\ll1$
(Appendix~\ref{app:amplification}). A pattern whose amplitude varies more
would win more often and have lower sign log-odds, the trend of
Figure~\ref{fig:competition}c. The pooled deficits do not show the order in $p/(N\gamma)$ expected from
this cause, but their intervals allow it (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}); we
made no direct test.

Both models are fully connected. The exact check covers linear ramps, one value of $NG$ and $a\le0.42$, so it
does not test that $c$ is independent of the field. The Hopfield test covers
one cooling rate, and its ratios do not test the equilibrium log-odds,
because the CW value enters both sides.

\paragraph{Related work.}
\label{sec:discussion}%
A cooling study of Ising chains \citep{suzuki2009} measures defects and
excess energy, not mode probabilities. Diffusion models with an exact score
reverse a noising process \citep{biroli2024}, so our calculation does not
cover them. There, a
variance-preserving process with too few uniform steps gets the mode
probabilities of a Gaussian mixture wrong, and dilated schedules recover
them, also for a two-mode CW distribution \citep{aranguri2025}. Annealed
importance sampling weights annealed runs to estimate equilibrium averages
\citep{neal2001}; we did not compare the two.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
In the CW model on linear ramps, a finite cooling rate fixes the mode
proportions of annealed Gibbs samples mainly in a short window around the
transition, and one formula gives the fraction of the log-odds that the
samples keep to within $2\%$. Three steps would extend it: runs at several
cooling rates in the Hopfield model, a theory of competition that holds for
single pattern sets, and the same calculation for diffusion samplers, which
needs their reverse dynamics.

\section{Derivation of the selection law}
\label{app:exact}

We derive Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} and the prefactor of Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling} in
four steps, and then define the exact chain that checks them.

\paragraph{Step 1: drift and noise of $m$.}
By Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}, a Gibbs update redraws spin $i$ with mean
$\tanh\ell_i$, where $\ell_i=\beta(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j\ne i}x_j+\beta G$. For
large $N$ this mean is approximately $\tanh(\beta m+\beta G)$. The chosen
spin has mean $m$ before its update, so per expected sweep
\[
 \dot m=-m+\tanh(\beta m+\beta G)\;\simeq\;(\beta-1)m+G ,
\]
where the second form holds for small $m$ and $G$ near $\beta=1$, and the
first term that it omits is $-m^3/3$. Near $m=0$, an update flips its spin
with probability about one half and a flip changes $m$ by $2/N$. The $N$
updates of an expected sweep therefore add a variance of about
$N\times\tfrac12\times(2/N)^2=2/N$, which we treat as Gaussian noise.

\paragraph{Step 2: divide out the growth.}
On a linear ramp, $\beta-1=\gamma t$, and Step~1 gives
$dm=(\gamma tm+G)\,dt+\sqrt{2/N}\,dW_t$, with $W$ a standard Brownian
motion. Without field or noise, $m$ is proportional to $e^{\gamma t^2/2}$. The quotient
$Y_t=e^{-\gamma t^2/2}m_t$ has the sign of $m_t$, and from a start at
$t_0<0$ it is
\[
 Y_t=e^{-\gamma t_0^2/2}m_{t_0}
       +G\int_{t_0}^t e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,du
       +\sqrt{2/N}\int_{t_0}^t e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,dW_u .
\]
The three terms are the remaining
initial imbalance, the accumulated field and the accumulated noise. The
weight $e^{-\gamma u^2/2}$ is appreciable only for $|u|$ of order
$\gamma^{-1/2}$, the selection window. If the ramp starts well below and
ends well above $\beta=1$, the first term is negligible and both integrals
can run over the real line. Their limit $Y$ is the early amplitude.

\paragraph{Step 3: the selection probability.}
The early amplitude is Gaussian, with mean and variance
\[
 \mu_Y=G\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,du=G\sqrt{2\pi/\gamma},
 \qquad
 V_Y=\frac2N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u^2}\,du
    =\frac2N\sqrt{\pi/\gamma}.
\]
In units of its standard deviation, it is $U=Y/\sqrt{V_Y}\sim\mathcal N(a,1)$
with
\[
 a=\frac{\mu_Y}{\sqrt{V_Y}}=G\sqrt N\,(\pi/\gamma)^{1/4}.
\]
A run ends with $m>0$ when $U>0$, which has probability $\Phi(a)$. This is
Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}.

\paragraph{Step 4: from the probability to $c$.}
For a weak field, both log-odds in $c$ are linear in $G$. For the samples,
$\Phi(a)\simeq1/2+a/\sqrt{2\pi}$, and $\logit q$ has slope $4$ at $q=1/2$. In
equilibrium at the final inverse temperature $\beta_f$, the field adds
$\beta_fNGm$ to the log weight of a configuration, which raises the log
probability of the positive mode by $\beta_fNG\bar m_f$ and lowers that of
the negative mode by the same amount. Here $\bar m_f$ is the zero-field mean
magnetization of the positive mode, close to the positive solution of
$m=\tanh(\beta_fm)$, which is $0.89$ at $\beta_f=1.6$. Hence
\[
 \logit\Phi(a)\simeq\frac{4a}{\sqrt{2\pi}},
 \qquad
 \logit\qeq\simeq2\beta_fNG\bar m_f,
 \qquad
 c\simeq\frac{\sqrt2}{\pi^{1/4}\beta_f\bar m_f}\,(N^2\gamma)^{-1/4}.
\]
The field cancels in the ratio, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling}. The
values of $c_{\mathrm{sel}}$ in Table~\ref{tab:linear} use the full form
$\logit\Phi(a)/\logit\qeq$, not this weak-field limit.

\paragraph{When the law holds.}
The steps need a slow ramp ($\gamma\ll1$) and a drift that stays linear until
the sign is set. The cubic term $m^3/3$ equals the linear term $\gamma tm$,
at the edge of the window, once $|m|$ is of order $\gamma^{1/4}$. The noise
accumulated in the window is a fraction $(N\gamma)^{-1/2}$ of this scale, so
$N\gamma\gg1$ keeps the drift nearly linear up to the edge of the window in
the zero-field case \citep{berglundgentz2002}. This condition also makes the
selection window $\sqrt\gamma$ wider than the finite-size critical window,
$|\beta-1|\sim N^{-1/2}$. The law does not need it at this $NG$: on the nine ramps below,
$N\gamma$ is $0.26$--$16$ and the law holds to $2\%$. The steps do not account for sign changes after the window. The switching times of
Section~\ref{sec:law} are mean passage times of the exact chain between the
mode centres, at fixed $\beta$ and zero field.

\paragraph{Exact chain.}
Updates arrive at rate $N$ per expected sweep, so the number $k$ of positive
spins is a continuous-time Markov chain. It rises or falls by one at a rate
equal to the number of eligible spins ($N-k$ negative or $k$ positive) times
their redraw probability:
\[
 W_k^+=\frac{N-k}{2}\Big[1+\tanh\!\Big(\beta\frac{2k-N+1}{N-1}+\beta G\Big)\Big],
 \qquad
 W_k^-=\frac{k}{2}\Big[1-\tanh\!\Big(\beta\frac{2k-N-1}{N-1}+\beta G\Big)\Big],
\]
where the $\pm1$ exclude the updated spin. We integrate the probabilities
$P_k(t)$ of the $N+1$ counts along the ramp with these rates (implicit Euler
steps of about $0.02$ sweeps with Richardson extrapolation; $\beta$ is held
at the midpoint of each interval $\Delta\beta=0.005$ for $0.005/\gamma$
sweeps), which gives $\qchain$. With $\pi_k$ the
equilibrium distribution of $k$ at the final $\beta$,
$\qeq=\sum_{k>N/2}\pi_k/\sum_{k\ne N/2}\pi_k$. The excluded state $m=0$ has
equilibrium probability below $10^{-36}$ at $\beta_f=1.6$ for $N\ge512$.

\paragraph{The nine ramps.}
The ramps of Table~\ref{tab:linear} run from equilibrium at $\beta=0.7$ to
$\beta=1.6$, with $G=0.0006125\times2048/N$, that is, $NG=1.2544$. A fixed
$NG$ keeps the equilibrium log-odds nearly constant across sizes. On the slowest ramp, $12\%$ ($N=1024$) and $3\%$ ($N=2048$) of runs still change the sign of $m$ after $\beta=1+3\sqrt\gamma$ (Section~\ref{sec:law}). The discrete chain of
Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}, which has no time step, reproduces the
$\gamma=0.004$ column as printed.

\section{The competition factor}
\label{app:amplification}

We derive $A_p$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification}. We then give the
predictions for one and two patterns, which need no Gaussian assumption, and
the condition under which random patterns violate the assumptions of $A_p$.

\paragraph{Derivation of $A_p$.}
Under the assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:test}, pattern~1's early amplitude
is $Y^1\sim\mathcal N(\delta,\sigma^2)$, where $\delta$ is the shift due to
the field, and the other amplitudes are independent
$\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. The sign of $Y^1$ is the final sign of $m^1$, and
$\delta/\sigma$ plays the role of $a$.

First, a larger magnitude makes the field more decisive. With $f$ the density
of $Y^1$, the odds of a positive sign at magnitude $r$ are
\[
 \frac{f(r)}{f(-r)}=e^{2r\delta/\sigma^2},
 \qquad\text{so}\qquad
 \Pr\big(Y^1>0\,\big|\,|Y^1|=r\big)\simeq\frac12+\frac{r\delta}{2\sigma^2}.
\]
Second, winning only selects large magnitudes. Which pattern wins depends
only on the magnitudes, so the probability above still holds among
the runs that pattern~1 wins. At $\delta=0$ the patterns are alike, and the
winning magnitude is distributed as $\sigma M_p$, where
$M_p=\max_{1\le\mu\le p}|Z_\mu|$ for independent standard Gaussians $Z_\mu$.
Reversing the field reverses the sign of $Y^1$ and leaves the magnitudes
unchanged in distribution, so the distribution of the winning magnitude given
that pattern~1 wins is an even function of $\delta$ and does not change to
first order. Third, we average the sign probability over
the winning magnitude:
\[
 \qchain\simeq\frac12+\frac{\delta}{2\sigma}\,\mathbb E M_p,
 \qquad
 \logit\qchain\simeq\frac{2\delta}{\sigma}\,\mathbb E M_p .
\]
With one pattern, $M_1=|Z|$, which is the weak-field result of
Appendix~\ref{app:exact}. The ratio of the two log-odds is
$A_p=\mathbb E M_p/\mathbb E|Z|$, where
$\mathbb E M_p=\int_0^\infty[1-(2\Phi(x)-1)^p]\,dx$, and $c_{\mathrm{pred}}=A_pc_{\mathrm{CW}}$
follows if the other patterns change neither $\delta/\sigma$ nor the
equilibrium log-odds.

\paragraph{One pattern.}
With $y_i=\xi_i^1x_i$, the Hopfield update of $x_i$ is the CW update of
$y_i$. Because the spin is chosen independently of the state, the one-pattern
sampler is the CW sampler for every $N$ and every pattern, and its $c$ is the
exact-chain value. This equality needs uniformly chosen spins. Permutation
sweeps, which redraw every spin once per sweep in random order, define a
different chain, which $k$ alone does not describe.

\paragraph{Two patterns.}
In the variables $y_i$, the coupling of $y_i$ and $y_j$ is
$(1+\eta_i\eta_j)/(N-1)$, with $\eta_i=\xi_i^1\xi_i^2$. It vanishes between
the spins on which the two patterns agree and those on which they differ, so
the model consists of two non-interacting CW blocks ($944$--$1104$ spins in
our pattern sets). With $S_A$ and $S_B$ the block sums of the $y_i$,
$Nm^1=S_A+S_B$ and $Nm^2=S_A-S_B$, so pattern~1 wins exactly when $S_A$ and
$S_B$ have the same sign, which is then the sign of $m^1$. We compute each
block's distribution exactly and treat the blocks as independent, which they
are up to the shared number of updates. Multiplying the blocks' odds and
pooling over pattern sets as the estimator does gives
$c_{\mathrm{pred}}=0.09363$ at $N=2048$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}, the sampled
log-odds of a block grow as the square root of its size, so two blocks of
$N/2$ spins give $\sqrt2$ times those of one block of $N$. This recovers
$A_2=\sqrt2$, and $A_2c_{\mathrm{CW}}=0.09316$ lies $0.5\%$ below the
two-block value.

\paragraph{When random patterns violate the assumptions.}
Let $C_{\mu\nu}=N^{-1}\sum_i\xi_i^\mu\xi_i^\nu$ be the correlation of
patterns $\mu$ and $\nu$. At zero field and for small overlaps, averaging the
expected Gibbs redraw over spins, weighted by $\xi_i^\mu$, gives to leading
order in $1/N$
\[
 \dot m^\mu\simeq-m^\mu+\beta\sum_\nu C_{\mu\nu}m^\nu .
\]
The component of $(m^1,\ldots,m^p)$ along an eigenvector of $C$ with
eigenvalue $\lambda$ therefore begins to grow at $\beta=1/\lambda$ and not at
$\beta=1$. An off-diagonal entry of $C$ averages $N$ independent signs and
has standard deviation $N^{-1/2}$, so the eigenvalues spread over a range of
order $(p/N)^{1/2}$. The selection window spans a $\beta$ range of order
$\sqrt\gamma$. The components start to grow at nearly the same point of the
window only when
\[
 (p/N)^{1/2}\ll\sqrt\gamma,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad p/(N\gamma)\ll1 .
\]
Otherwise the components have unequal growth factors. Each overlap is a
mixture of the components, and the amplitudes of the patterns can then have
unequal variances. Under the largest-amplitude rule, a pattern whose amplitude varies more wins more often, and
the sets in which pattern~1 won more often had a lower $c$ (Table~\ref{tab:sets}). Before the
runs, an unvalidated linear Gaussian model of these components gave expected
deficits of $0.5$--$3\%$; we measured $0.5$--$6\%$.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
A written protocol fixed the predictions, the estimator, the run counts and
how to read the four ratios $c/(A_pc_{\mathrm{CW}})$ with $p\ge3$. It
distinguishes three readings:
\begin{center}
 \begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.44\textwidth}p{0.40\textwidth}@{}}
 & Condition on the four ratios & Conclusion\\[2pt]
 (a) & Every $95\%$ interval contains $1$. & $A_p$ holds to within $3\%$, the planned half-width.\\
 (b) & Some intervals lie below $1$, and the deficits grow with $p/(N\gamma)$. & $A_p$ is the limit $N\gamma\to\infty$.\\
 (c) & Some interval lies above $1$, or the deficits are not ordered by $p/(N\gamma)$. & An assumption other than common variance fails; $A_p$ is not supported as derived.\\
 \end{tabular}
\end{center}
Reading (c) applies. The largest deficit is at $p=4$, $N=2048$, whereas
$p/(N\gamma)$ is largest at $p=3$, $N=1024$, and the rank correlation of the
four deficits with $p/(N\gamma)$ is $0.6$, although the four
intervals overlap. The rows with $p=3$ and $p=4$ at $N=2048$ share their
pattern sets, and on these sets the pooled four-pattern ratio is $0.055$ lower
(post hoc; paired bootstrap interval $[0.008,0.099]$, above zero at all $200$
seeds), which agrees with the order of $p/(N\gamma)$ for this pair. Reading (a), which concerns the intervals and not the order, also applies at the protocol's bootstrap seed, where the
four-pattern interval ends at $1.0005$, but that interval ends below $1$ at
$139$ of the $200$ bootstrap seeds $0$--$199$. We report reading (c), the
less favorable one. The post hoc win shares (Table~\ref{tab:sets}) show
unlike patterns, which the protocol did not attribute to this reading.

Some facts qualify the protocol. It is committed to the repository
(\texttt{docs/}) together with all seeds, the pilot and the results, so no external record shows that
it preceded the runs. A pilot with a tenth of the planned runs, never pooled
with the results, failed both of its one-pattern checks at $95\%$, and four
replicates with new seeds and more runs passed (ratios $0.970$--$1.016$). Before any reported run,
we doubled all run counts and chose to read the four controls at a joint
$95\%$ level; their separate $95\%$ intervals also contain the exact values
(Table~\ref{tab:transfer}). Three of the four results with $p\ge3$ missed the
planned half-width of $3\%$, because the sets varied more than assumed.

\paragraph{Pattern sets (post hoc).}
The analysis of single pattern sets is not in the protocol. For each set,
with field signs pooled, we compute the share of runs that pattern~1 wins and
$\widehat c_s$, the estimator applied to that set alone
(Table~\ref{tab:sets}). The pooled $\widehat c$ weights each set by the runs
that pattern~1 won, hence most heavily the sets with a large win share and a
low $\widehat c_s$; the last column of the table weights the sets equally. For the controls, the chi-square test of Table~\ref{tab:sets}
finds no excess spread ($P=0.56$--$0.94$), so the spread
for $p\ge3$ does not come from the sampler or the estimator.
