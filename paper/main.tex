\begin{abstract}
A sampling algorithm can generate plausible examples from a probability model
but in the wrong proportions. We study this error in annealed Gibbs sampling,
which updates individual variables while lowering the temperature. In the
Curie--Weiss (CW) model, binary variables tend to share one sign. A weak field
favors $+1$ but competes with random fluctuations as cooling chooses the sign.
Classical theory predicts this choice; comparing it with the equilibrium
probabilities gives a law for how much of the field's preference
survives. Much slower cooling raises the surviving preference only modestly.
The law agrees closely with numerical evolution of the exact CW chain. In a
Hopfield model with several stored patterns, we remove runs in one mode after
brief cooling and reheat the rest so that they choose again. Competition
amplifies the preference between a pattern and its negative. A Gaussian
approximation captures this effect, with discrepancies across pattern counts,
sizes, and update rules. The results relate the sign chosen during cooling to
final mode probabilities; the CW calculation is a reference for comparing
cooling schedules.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Suppose a model of handwritten digits assigns twice as much probability to
``3'' as to ``8''. A sampling algorithm should then produce
about twice as many ``3''s as ``8''s over many runs. It can produce
recognizable examples of both digits and still get this proportion wrong.
Probabilities and averages estimated from these samples can then be biased. How
does the sampling algorithm change these proportions?

We study annealed Gibbs sampling. A Gibbs update redraws one variable from its
conditional distribution given the other variables. High temperature gives a
broad distribution; lower temperature concentrates probability around likely
outcomes. Cooling, or annealing, lowers the temperature to its target value.
During cooling, moving between groups of likely outcomes becomes difficult.
Further updates within one group need not correct how often each group was
chosen \citep{neal2001}.

To isolate this error from inaccuracies in a learned model, we use the
Curie--Weiss (CW) model. Its binary variables tend to align, so cooling makes
most of them share one sign. A weak field favors the positive sign but
competes with random fluctuations while cooling selects the sign. Both the
model's probabilities and the evolution of the sampling algorithm can be
computed from the number of positive variables alone.

We connect a classical prediction of this sign choice
\citep{kondepudinelson1983,kondepudigao1987,nene2018} to the error in final
proportions. The resulting law quantifies how much of the field's preference
survives cooling and shows why much slower cooling improves it only modestly.
Extending the argument to a Hopfield model with several stored binary patterns
predicts that competition among patterns strengthens this preference. We test
the prediction on runs reheated to choose a mode again.\footnote{Code, saved runs,
the full protocol, and material omitted here (relaxation times, the full $p=2$
calculation, an MNIST illustration):
\url{https://github.com/ali-izhar/finite-rate-selection-law}.} It closely matches
three-pattern simulations but misses by up to $11\%$ of the prediction at
other pattern counts or sizes.

\section{An exact model of mode selection}
\label{sec:model}

A configuration is a vector $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$ of binary variables
$x_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, called spins. The CW model assigns higher probability
to configurations in which spins agree:
\begin{equation}
 \pi_{\beta,G}(x)\propto
 \exp\!\left[\frac{\beta}{2(N-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}x_i x_j
              +\beta G\sum_i x_i\right].
 \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
At $\beta=0$, all configurations are equally probable. Increasing $\beta$
strengthens the preference for alignment and corresponds to cooling;
$\beta$ is therefore the \emph{inverse temperature}. The \emph{field} $G$
favors spins of its sign and stays fixed during each cooling run.
At fixed temperature, Gibbs updates eventually reproduce $\pi_{\beta,G}$,
the \emph{equilibrium distribution}.

The average spin, $m=N^{-1}\sum_i x_i$, is the \emph{magnetization}.
Its sign separates mostly positive from mostly negative configurations.
Without a field, in the limit $N\to\infty$, the equilibrium magnetization
concentrates at zero for $\beta<1$ and at two opposite nonzero values for
$\beta>1$, with the \emph{transition} at $\beta=1$. The configurations around these
two values form two \emph{modes}, or groups of likely outcomes.

For CW, each Gibbs update chooses a spin uniformly. Updates occur at random
times at rate $N$; one unit of time, $N$ updates on average, is an
\emph{expected sweep}.
All configurations with the same number $k$ of positive spins have the same
rates of changing $k$. We can therefore track $N+1$ probabilities, one for
each $k=0,\ldots,N$, instead of $2^N$ configurations \citep{levinluczak2010}, and evolve them
numerically (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).

Let $\qchain$ be the probability that the algorithm ends with $m>0$, and
$\qeq$ the probability of $m>0$ under the final equilibrium distribution,
both conditioned on $m\ne0$. For a weak field, sampled and equilibrium
log-odds are both approximately proportional to $G$ (Section~\ref{sec:law}). Their ratio
measures the surviving preference:
\begin{equation}
 c=\frac{\logit\qchain}{\logit\qeq},
 \qquad \logit q=\log\frac{q}{1-q},
 \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
for $\qeq\ne1/2$. $c=1$ reproduces the equilibrium preference,
whereas $c=0$ gives equal mode probabilities. If equilibrium odds are
$4{:}1$, then $c=0.2$ gives sampled odds of
$4^{0.2}{:}1\approx1.32{:}1$.

\section{How much of the preference survives?}
\label{sec:law}

The sign chosen near $\beta=1$ can determine the final mode probabilities.
There, a small random imbalance between positive and negative spins
can grow into either mode (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}a).
The field biases this early choice.
After the modes form, switching between them can take much longer
than settling within either one \citep{levinluczak2010,dlpcmp2009}.

Near $m=0$ and $\beta=1$, the mean rate of change of $m$, or
\emph{drift}, is approximately \mbox{$(\beta-1)m+G$}. The first term reduces
an imbalance below $\beta=1$ and amplifies it above.
Random Gibbs updates add \emph{noise}, with variance
approximately $2/N$ per expected sweep (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).
Let $\beta(t)=1+\gamma t$ increase at a constant rate $\gamma>0$ and
cross the transition at $t=0$. The growth exponent of $m$,
$\int_0^t\gamma u\,du=\gamma t^2/2$, reaches order one
after a time $T$ of order $\gamma^{-1/2}$. In this time, the field
contributes an imbalance of order $GT$, while the noise accumulates
as $\sqrt{T/N}$ \citep{berglundgentz2002}. Their ratio is of order
$G\sqrt N\,\gamma^{-1/4}$. Slower cooling therefore favors the field.

\begin{samepage}
Dividing $m$ by the positive growth factor $e^{\gamma t^2/2}$ of the linear
drift preserves its sign and gives the early \emph{amplitude}: the
accumulated field plus noise.
In the linear approximation, the noise is a weighted sum of independent
Gaussian increments. The amplitude is therefore Gaussian; dividing it by
its standard deviation gives $U$, whose mean $a$ measures the field
relative to the noise. A classical
calculation \citep[][Appendix~\ref{app:exact}]{kondepudinelson1983,kondepudigao1987} gives the probability
$q_{\mathrm{sel}}$ of selecting $m>0$:
\begin{equation}
 q_{\mathrm{sel}}\simeq\Phi(a),\qquad a=G\sqrt N(\pi/\gamma)^{1/4},
 \label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard Gaussian distribution function
(Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}b). Without later switching, $q_{\mathrm{sel}}$ also
gives $\qchain$.\par
\end{samepage}

 The blue area is $q_{\mathrm{sel}}=\Phi(a)$.}
 \label{fig:mechanism}

When $|a|\ll1$, both signs are nearly equally likely,
$\logit\Phi(a)\approx4a/\sqrt{2\pi}$, and sampled log-odds grow as
$G\sqrt N\,\gamma^{-1/4}$. In equilibrium, reversing all $N$ spins changes
the field term of the log-probability by order $NG$. At a fixed final
temperature, equilibrium log-odds thus grow as $NG$. Dividing gives
\begin{equation}
 c\propto(N^2\gamma)^{-1/4}.
 \label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
Sixteenfold longer cooling doubles $c$
in this regime, and fourfold larger $N$ halves it.

The \emph{selection law}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} converted to $c$ by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:c}, assumes a slow ramp ($\gamma\ll1$), a start well below
$\beta=1$, and little switching after selection. A sufficient condition
for the noise to stay below $|m|\sim\gamma^{1/4}$, where growth stops being linear, is $N\gamma\gg1$
\citep{berglundgentz2002} (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).
At fixed $N$, the law does not extend to arbitrarily slow cooling: long
sampling restores the equilibrium mode probabilities, but at $N=2048$ a
zero-field switch between modes takes on average about $5\times10^{7}$
expected sweeps at $\beta=1.1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).

\paragraph{Exact check.}
The selection law agrees with numerical CW evolution over the whole
cooling run. Runs start from equilibrium at $\beta=0.7$ and cool
to $1.6$ with $G=0.0006125\times2048/N$; fixed $NG$ keeps the equilibrium
log-odds nearly constant across sizes. For $N=1024$--$4096$ and
$\gamma=0.00025$--$0.004$, the numerical $c$ exceeds the prediction in all nine cases, by
$0.23$--$1.81\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}a; Table~\ref{tab:linear}).
Agreement holds even at the smallest tested $N\gamma=0.256$,
where $N\gamma\gg1$ fails.

\section{Competition among stored patterns}
\label{sec:test}

\paragraph{Hopfield model.}
A Hopfield model stores $p$
patterns $\xi^\mu\in\{-1,+1\}^N$ with independent equiprobable entries.
A configuration $x$ has probability proportional to
$e^{-\beta E_G(x)}$, with \emph{energy}
\begin{equation}
 E_G(x)=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i\ne j}\sum_{\mu=1}^p
       \xi_i^\mu\xi_j^\mu x_i x_j-G\sum_i\xi_i^1x_i.
 \label{eq:hopfield}
\end{equation}
At low temperature and $p\ll N$, configurations concentrate near a stored
pattern or its negative \citep{ags1985}. The \emph{overlap}
$m^\mu=N^{-1}\sum_i\xi_i^\mu x_i$ measures this resemblance: it is $+1$
at pattern $\mu$ and $-1$ at its negative. Each sample is labeled $(\mu,\pm)$ by the
pattern with largest $|m^\mu|$ and the sign of that overlap. Here $\qchain$ and $\qeq$
are positive-sign probabilities among samples assigned to pattern 1, not pattern
frequencies.

Competition with other patterns strengthens the field's preference for
pattern 1 over its negative. Suppose the early overlaps are
independent Gaussians with a common variance, the field shifts only the mean
of $m^1$ (as in the CW model), and the largest $|m^\mu|$ determines the
final pattern.
For a shifted Gaussian, the sign log-odds at a given magnitude grow linearly in that magnitude. Winning selects larger magnitudes and so
strengthens the sign preference. To first order in the field, averaging over winning magnitudes relative to one pattern (the CW case) gives
\begin{equation}
 A_p=\frac{\mathbb E\max_{1\le\mu\le p}|Z_\mu|}{\mathbb E|Z|},
 \qquad c_{\mathrm{pred}}=A_p c_{\mathrm{CW}},
 \label{eq:amplification}
\end{equation}
where $Z$ and the $Z_\mu$ are independent standard Gaussians and
$c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ is $c$ from exact CW evolution of the Hopfield cooling
protocol (next paragraph), not of a linear ramp.
The factors are $A_1=1$, $A_2=\sqrt2$, $A_3=1.662$, and $A_4=1.836$
(Appendix~\ref{app:amplification}). The prediction also assumes equal Hopfield and CW equilibrium sign
preferences; a small $p/N$ guarantees neither this equality nor independent overlaps.

\paragraph{Reheating after a filtered start.}
Reheating lets a run that settled away from the favored mode choose again
(\emph{reselection}). We test the prediction here, not on fresh
cooling, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling} does not apply directly.
A \emph{permutation sweep} updates every spin
once in a fresh random order ($N$ updates, like an expected sweep).
The \emph{cooling schedule} has ten inverse temperatures from $0.6$ to $1.6$;
each dwell is six relaxation times of the zero-field CW chain, across both
signs for $\beta\le1$ and within one sign above (Table~\ref{tab:schedule}); in one relaxation time, the slowest deviation from equilibrium shrinks $e$-fold. Its $834$ sweeps, computed at $N=2048$, are reused
at every size.

Each run has three stages. First, a brief cooling through the schedule at
two sweeps per temperature; removing runs labeled $(1,+)$ leaves a
\emph{filtered start}; for $G>0$, it imitates a sampler that settled away
from the favored mode. Second, a \emph{reheat} for $30$ sweeps at $\beta=0.7$, where the drift
reduces an imbalance. Third, the \emph{final cooling} through the
schedule. The removal also applies at $G<0$, so fields $\pm G$ follow one
protocol. From a sign-symmetric start, $\qchain(G)+\qchain(-G)=1$; memory
of the removal could break this. The measured sums are consistent with $1$
but do not establish equivalence to fresh starts
(Table~\ref{tab:counts}).
CW evolution of this protocol gives $c_{\mathrm{CW}}=0.120$ at $N=2048$; scaling final cooling by $0.25$ to $4$ (sixteenfold) raises it $1.48$-fold, not the twofold of Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling} (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).

\paragraph{Result.}
For three patterns and $N=2048$, the positive sign occurs in $66.9\%$ of
samples assigned to pattern 1 at $G=0.0006125$, against $97.26\%$ in the
equilibrium reference; their logits give $c=0.70/3.57\approx0.20$.
Unlike CW, this reference is approximate: its log-odds $2\beta_f NG\bar m^1$
use the zero-field mean positive-mode overlap $\bar m^1$ at the final $\beta_f$
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

At all eight nonzero fields of the \emph{field grid}, the sampled log-odds are approximately
proportional to the reference log-odds (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b).
Slopes fitted to twenty independently drawn pattern sets, weighted by the
number of samples assigned to pattern 1, average to
$c=0.199$ ($95\%$ interval $[0.1908,0.2095]$ from resampling pattern
sets). Equal pattern-set weights give $0.2101$, just above this interval; three other estimators give $0.1994$--$0.1997$ (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). The prediction $A_3c_{\mathrm{CW}}=0.199$ uses no
Hopfield samples and agrees closely, but approximation errors may cancel
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Where the prediction departs}
\label{sec:limits}

Other settings miss the prediction by up to $11\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}; for $p=2$, see Appendix~\ref{app:amplification}).
They lack a Hopfield reference, so they use the exact CW reference and,
assuming $\qchain(G)+\qchain(-G)=1$, pool positive outcomes at $+G$
with negative ones at $-G$ (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).
For $p=3$ this gives $0.201$; the field-grid fit gives $0.199$.

The close three-pattern match may come partly from cancelling errors.
One pattern removes competition, yet $c$ exceeds $c_{\mathrm{CW}}$; separate
one-pattern runs give $c=1.063\,c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ at $N=2048$ (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). The models differ in update
order (permutation versus expected sweeps) and interaction strength ($1/N$
versus $1/(N-1)$). Multiplying
the $p=3$ prediction by $1.063$ gives $0.212$, outside the field-grid interval.

Measured $c$ also falls more slowly with size than predicted: as
$N^{-0.36}$ versus $N^{-0.46}$ over $N=1024$--$4096$ (two-point slopes, no
uncertainties; Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling} gives $N^{-1/2}$ for a fixed ramp).
These fixed-schedule comparisons do not measure the rate dependence of
Hopfield sampling. Measured and predicted $c$ cross near $N=2048$, where the
schedule was set. Scaled by the one-pattern ratio at
each size ($1.126$, $1.020$), the prediction becomes $0.319$ at $N=1024$ and $0.153$ at $4096$,
so the trend remains: measured $c$ is $18\%$ below and $4\%$ above (unrounded values).

Pattern correlation is one untested explanation of the size trend. Random
patterns have correlations of order $N^{-1/2}$, which shift the inverse
temperatures at which overlaps begin to grow by the same order as the
\emph{critical window}, the range over which fluctuations spread the transition
at finite $N$. The shifts need not become negligible relative to this window as $N$
grows (Appendix~\ref{app:amplification}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The selection law links errors in mode probabilities to the field and noise
as the modes form; Ising-chain cooling studies \citep{suzuki2009} instead
track misaligned regions and excess energy.

Annealed importance sampling weights annealed runs to estimate equilibrium averages
\citep{neal2001}. An equal-cost comparison with reheating
would need valid weights for the filtered start and an assessment of estimator
bias and variance.
Diffusion models change their distribution gradually, but with an exact score
they reverse a noising process and need not equilibrate at each step
\citep{biroli2024}; extending the calculation needs their
dynamics and discrete updates. A variance-preserving noise
schedule can miss mode asymmetry; suitable ones recover mode probabilities and within-mode
variation in Gaussian mixtures and CW \citep{aranguri2025}.

CW is an exactly computable test case for comparing cooling schedules by their
error in mode probabilities; applying $c$ elsewhere requires an equilibrium reference.
Measuring Hopfield overlaps during cooling could reveal whether pattern
correlations explain the remaining discrepancies.

\section{Selection formula and exact-chain calculations}
\label{app:exact}

\paragraph{From spin updates to drift and noise.}
For a chosen spin, let $\ell_i=\beta(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j\ne i}x_j+h$,
where $h=\beta G$. Holding the other spins fixed, Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}
assigns weights $e^{\ell_i}$ and $e^{-\ell_i}$ to its two values, so the
redrawn spin has mean $\tanh\ell_i$. For large $N$, the neighbors' average is approximately $m$.
A uniformly chosen spin has old mean $m$, so one update changes $m$ by
$[\tanh(\beta m+h)-m]/N$ in expectation. With $N$ updates per expected sweep,
the drift is $\dot m=-m+\tanh(\beta m+h)$.
Near $m=0$ and in a weak field, an update flips a spin with probability about
one half, and each flip changes $m$ by $2/N$. The noise variance per expected sweep
is therefore approximately $N\times\tfrac12\times(2/N)^2=2/N$.
A short interval contains many updates that change $m$ little; we
approximate the centered sum of their jumps by a Gaussian.
Expanding $\tanh z=z-z^3/3+\cdots$ near $\beta=1$, retaining the field,
linear, and cubic terms, and writing $G$ for $h=\beta G$ (equal at $\beta=1$) gives
\begin{equation}
 dm=\big[(\beta-1)m-m^3/3+G\big]dt+\sqrt{2/N}\,dW_t,
 \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion.
At $G=0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:sde} is the swept pitchfork bifurcation analyzed by
\citet{berglundgentz2002}: one stable value becomes two.

\paragraph{Removing growth without changing the sign.}
For the linear ramp, $\beta-1=\gamma t$. While $m$ is small, omit the cubic
term. Without field or noise, $\dot m=\gamma t m$ has the positive growth
factor $e^{\gamma t^2/2}$. Set $Y_t=e^{-\gamma t^2/2}m_t$ and start at
$t_0<0$. The product rule gives
\[
 Y_t=e^{-\gamma t_0^2/2}m_{t_0}
       +G\int_{t_0}^t e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,du
       +\sqrt{2/N}\int_{t_0}^t e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,dW_u.
\]
The three terms are the remaining initial imbalance, the accumulated field,
and the accumulated noise. With $w(u)=e^{-\gamma u^2/2}$, the noise integral
is the limit of sums $\sum_j w(u_j)\sqrt{\Delta u}\,\epsilon_j$ of independent
standard Gaussians $\epsilon_j$: a Gaussian with mean zero and variance
$\sum_j w(u_j)^2\Delta u$. The field term therefore uses $w$, and the noise
variance uses $w^2$.

The weights are appreciable over times of order $\gamma^{-1/2}$ around
the transition. An early start suppresses the initial term; a late end
lets both integrals settle. Extending their limits to the whole real line
gives the amplitude $Y$ with
\begin{align*}
 \mu_Y=\mathbb E Y
 &=G\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,du
   =G\sqrt{2\pi/\gamma},\\
 V_Y=\operatorname{Var}(Y)
 &=\frac2N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u^2}\,du
   =\frac2N\sqrt{\pi/\gamma}.
\end{align*}
Hence $U=Y/\sqrt{V_Y}=a+Z$, where $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and
$a=\mu_Y/\sqrt{V_Y}=G\sqrt N(\pi/\gamma)^{1/4}$.
The positive mode is selected when $U>0$, with probability
$\Pr(Z>-a)=\Phi(a)$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}.
These infinite limits approximate a finite cooling interval; the integrals must settle before the cubic term
substantially changes the amplitude.

\paragraph{From a sign probability to retained log-odds.}
For small $a$, $\Phi(a)=1/2+a/\sqrt{2\pi}+O(a^3)$.
The derivative of $\logit q$ at $q=1/2$ is $4$, so
$\logit\Phi(a)=4a/\sqrt{2\pi}+O(a^3)$.
At the final inverse temperature $\beta_f$, a field adds $\beta_f NGm$
to each configuration's log weight. To first order in $G$, the positive
mode's log mass changes by its average, $\beta_f NG\bar m_f$, and the
negative mode's by the opposite amount. Here $\bar m_f$ is the zero-field
mean magnetization in the positive mode at $\beta_f$.
The equilibrium log-odds is thus approximately $2\beta_f NG\bar m_f$.
Dividing the two leading log-odds gives
\[
 c\simeq\frac{\sqrt2\,\pi^{-1/4}}{\beta_f\bar m_f}
             (N^2\gamma)^{-1/4}.
\]

\paragraph{Regime.}
Over the selection time $T\sim\gamma^{-1/2}$, the linear coefficient
$\gamma T$ is of order $\sqrt\gamma$, so the cubic term matters once
$|m|\sim\gamma^{1/4}$. Noise accumulated over $T$ has typical magnitude
$\sqrt{T/N}\sim N^{-1/2}\gamma^{-1/4}$, a fraction $(N\gamma)^{-1/2}$ of
this scale. The field contribution must also be small on this scale,
$|G|T/\gamma^{1/4}\sim |G|\gamma^{-3/4}\ll1$, which follows from
$N\gamma\gg1$ if $a$ remains bounded.
At equilibrium, Stirling's formula for the $\binom Nk$ configurations with
$m=2k/N-1$, plus the interaction $N\beta m^2/2$, gives to leading order in $N$
$\log\pi(m)\simeq\mathrm{constant}+N[(\beta-1)m^2/2-m^4/12]$.
At $\beta=1$ the quadratic terms cancel, so fluctuations reach
$|m|\sim N^{-1/4}$; the quadratic term matters when $|\beta-1|\sim N^{-1/2}$,
the critical window of Section~\ref{sec:limits}. Selection spans an
inverse-temperature range $\gamma T\sim\sqrt\gamma$; requiring it to be
wider than this window again gives $N\gamma\gg1$.
These conditions allow linear selection before nonlinear growth fixes
the magnitude; they do not exclude later switching.

\paragraph{Exact reduction and numerical evolution.}
Waiting times between CW updates are independent exponentials with mean
$1/N$, so the count $k$ of positive spins is a Markov chain whose rates, at
a given temperature, depend only on $k$. Each spin updates once per expected
sweep on average, so each rate is the number of eligible spins ($N-k$
negative or $k$ positive) times their redraw probability:
\begin{align}
 W_k^+&=\frac{N-k}{2}\left[1+\tanh\!\left(\beta\frac{2k-N+1}{N-1}+h\right)\right],\\
 W_k^-&=\frac{k}{2}\left[1-\tanh\!\left(\beta\frac{2k-N-1}{N-1}+h\right)\right],
 \label{eq:rates}
\end{align}
where $h=\beta G$; the $+1$ and $-1$ exclude the updated spin from the
average of its neighbors. Equal probability flow between adjacent counts,
$\pi_kW_k^+=\pi_{k+1}W_{k+1}^-$ (\emph{detailed balance}), holds for
\begin{equation}
 \pi_k\propto\binom Nk
 \exp\!\left[\frac{\beta}{2(N-1)}\big((2k-N)^2-N\big)+h(2k-N)\right],
 \label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
where the binomial factor counts configurations with the same $k$.
The conditional reference is
$\qeq=\sum_{k>N/2}\pi_k/\sum_{k\ne N/2}\pi_k$;
the omitted zero-magnetization mass is below $10^{-36}$ at $\beta_f=1.6$ for
$N\ge512$.
We evolve the count probabilities $P_k(t)$,
\[
 \dot P_k=W_{k-1}^+P_{k-1}+W_{k+1}^-P_{k+1}-(W_k^++W_k^-)P_k,
\]
by implicit Euler steps with Richardson extrapolation. The result therefore
has no sampling error.

\paragraph{Fixed schedule.}
Mirroring the Hopfield protocol, the CW evolution starts with
$\operatorname{Binomial}(N,1/2)$ counts, runs two sweeps at each temperature of
Table~\ref{tab:schedule}, removes $k\ge N/2$ (including $m=0$) and renormalizes,
reheats for $30$ sweeps at $\beta=0.7$, then applies Table~\ref{tab:schedule}.
The removal
keeps $k<N/2$ for both field signs, as the Hopfield runs remove $(1,+)$ for
both, so the two logits in $c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ share the sign of $G$.
Switching after selection is unlikely while each dwell is much shorter than the
mean time for the exact zero-field chain to pass between mode centers: at
$N=2048$, about $10^{7.7}$, $10^{22.9}$, and $10^{137.8}$ expected
sweeps at $\beta=1.1$, $1.2$, and $1.6$, against $834$ sweeps in the whole schedule.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling} describes a constant-rate ramp from a start well below
$\beta=1$, whereas this schedule dwells at ten fixed temperatures after a
filtered start and reheating; we did not isolate which difference makes
sixteenfold final cooling raise $c$ less than twofold (Table~\ref{tab:schedule}).

\paragraph{Schedule design.}
The relaxation time $\tau$ is the inverse \emph{spectral gap}, the smallest
positive decay rate of the chain at fixed temperature: in time $\tau$, the
slowest deviation from equilibrium shrinks by $e^{-1}$. The six-$\tau$ dwell of
Table~\ref{tab:schedule} is a relaxation prescription, not a bound on the error
in final mode probabilities. To measure relaxation within a sign, the
\emph{reflected} chain (even $N$) keeps $k=N/2,\ldots,N$, sets $W^-_{N/2}=0$,
and leaves the other rates unchanged. Below the transition, the schedule uses the full
chain, because reflection would remove the slow relaxation of the balance between signs.
At $\beta=1$ and $G=0$, the full-chain inverse gap is $42.2$, $59.0$, and $82.7$ sweeps
for $N=1024$, $2048$, and $4096$; it grows as $\sqrt N$, so dwells redesigned for each $N$ would too.

\section{Simulation and statistical methods}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Sampling and labels.}
Each update redraws $x_i$ with probability
$\Pr(x_i=+1\mid x_{-i})=[1+\tanh(\beta H_i)]/2$, where $x_{-i}$ denotes the other spins and
$H_i=N^{-1}\sum_\mu\xi_i^\mu\sum_j\xi_j^\mu x_j-(p/N)x_i+G\xi_i^1$;
the term $-(p/N)x_i$ removes self-interaction. All runs begin with
independent unbiased spins, and the field is present during the
initial cooling, reheating, and final cooling. Only final samples labeled
pattern 1 with nonzero sign (\emph{retained} samples) enter the estimate.
Every nonzero-field point's sample interval lies strictly between $1/2$ and its reference
interval; all eight nonzero fields of Table~\ref{tab:counts} enter the fit, and
none was excluded.
Table~\ref{tab:runs} lists seeds and run counts.

\paragraph{Equilibrium reference.}
For each pattern set $s$, $256$ runs start at $+\xi^1$ and run at
$\beta_f=1.6$, $G=0$. After $60$ equilibration sweeps, overlaps are recorded
for $200$ sweeps; all measurements were labeled $(1,+)$.
Their mean $\bar m^1_s$ gives the weak-field reference
$\logit q_{\mathrm{eq},s}(G)\approx2\beta_f NG\bar m^1_s$.
The standard error (SE) divides the standard deviation of the pattern set's
$256\times200$ overlaps by
$\sqrt{256\times200/\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}}$, with $\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}=1.6946$ the reflected CW
relaxation time at $N=2048$, $\beta=1.6$, $G=0$.
$\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is an approximate correlation correction, fixed before the runs,
not a measured Hopfield correlation time.
The standard error of the $256$ independent run means, which needs no correlation time,
is $0.85$--$1.05$ times the reported SE for each pattern set.
The log-odds envelope adds the statistical term $1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$ and a
$1\%$ systematic allowance for the reference, a design choice rather than a
measured bound:
\[
 \Delta L=2\beta_f N|G|\big[1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(\bar m^1)+0.01\bar m^1\big].
\]
The pooled references in Table~\ref{tab:counts} and
Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b weight pattern set $s$ by $\omega_s=n_s(G)/\sum_r n_r(G)$,
with $n_s(G)$ its retained samples at field $G$:
$\bar m^1=\sum_s \omega_s\bar m^1_s$ and
$\mathrm{SE}(\bar m^1)^2=\sum_s \omega_s^2\mathrm{SE}(\bar m^1_s)^2$.
In Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b, this envelope (both terms) gives the horizontal error bars, and pooled
$95\%$ Wilson intervals give the vertical ones.
A common $1\%$ increase in the overlaps reduces $c$ by about $1\%$.
The bootstrap excludes the allowance and
error in the Hopfield equilibrium approximation.
For the CW reference at $N=2048$, $\beta=1.6$, the exact and weak-field $\qeq$
differ by at most $2.108\times10^{-8}$ over the eight fields, negligible
relative to the observed distortion.

\paragraph{Slope estimate.}
For field $j$ and pattern set $s$, let $x_{js}=\logit q_{\mathrm{eq},js}$,
$y_{js}=\logit\widehat q_{js}$ with $\widehat q_{js}=n_{+,js}/n_{js}$, and
$[L_{js},U_{js}]$ the $95\%$ Wilson interval of $\widehat q_{js}$ \citep{wilson1927}.
Each pattern set's slope weights its measurements by the inverse squared logit half-width
of this interval:
\[
 w_{js}=\left[\frac{\logit U_{js}-\logit L_{js}}{2\times1.96}\right]^{-2},
 \qquad c_s=\frac{\sum_j w_{js}x_{js}y_{js}}{\sum_j w_{js}x_{js}^2}.
\]
The grid estimate is $\widehat c_{\mathrm{grid}}=\sum_s n_sc_s/\sum_s n_s$,
with $n_s$ the retained samples over the set's eight fields.
Resampling the twenty pattern sets $20{,}000$ times, with reference means $\bar m^1_s$ held fixed,
gives $\widehat c_{\mathrm{grid}}=0.1994$ and the
$2.5$th--$97.5$th percentile interval $[0.1908,0.2095]$.
Three alternative estimators give $0.1994$--$0.1997$: weights
$n_{js}\tilde q_{js}(1-\tilde q_{js})$, with $\tilde q_{js}$ the Wilson-interval center;
all fields pooled before fitting; and a pooled binomial logistic fit
$\logit q=\alpha+cx$ (intercept $0.000944$).

\paragraph{Pattern-count and size comparisons.}
With $n_{+,s}(G)$ and $n_s(G)$ the positive and total retained counts of
pattern set $s$ at field $G$, let
\[
 S_s=n_{+,s}(+G)+n_s(-G)-n_{+,s}(-G),\qquad
 R_s=n_s(+G)+n_s(-G).
\]
The pooled estimator
$\widehat c_{\mathrm{scan}}=\logit(\sum_s S_s/\sum_s R_s)/\logit q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathrm{CW}}(N)$
uses the CW reference in place of the Hopfield one and does not fit a slope
per pattern set. The $p=3$, $N=2048$ row of Table~\ref{tab:transfer} comes from separate runs on
twelve of the field grid's pattern sets ($s=3$--$14$).
One-pattern runs ($s=0$; full protocol with the filtered start; $|G|=0.0006125$)
give $c$ equal to $1.065$, $1.126$, $1.063$, and $1.020$ times
$c_{\mathrm{CW}}=0.227$, $0.171$, $0.120$, $0.090$ at $N=512$, $1024$, $2048$, $4096$.

\section{Amplification and pattern correlations}
\label{app:amplification}

\paragraph{Conditioning on the winning pattern.}
Under the independent-Gaussian approximation, let
$m^1\sim\mathcal N(\delta,\sigma^2)$, where $\delta$ is the field-induced
mean shift and the other overlaps have zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$.
The density $f$ of $m^1$ gives $f(r)/f(-r)=\exp(2r\delta/\sigma^2)$ at
magnitude $r>0$.
Winning depends only on the magnitude, so it does not change these sign
odds at fixed $r$:
\[
 \Pr(m^1>0\mid |m^1|=r,\ \text{pattern 1 wins})
 =\frac{1}{1+e^{-2r\delta/\sigma^2}}
 \simeq\frac12+\frac{r\delta}{2\sigma^2}.
\]
At $\delta=0$, all patterns have the same distribution, so the
winning magnitude is distributed as $\sigma M_p$, where
$M_p=\max_{1\le\mu\le p}|Z_\mu|$ for independent standard Gaussians $Z_\mu$.
The magnitude distribution is even in $\delta$, so its first-order change is zero.
Averaging the sign probability over winning magnitudes then gives, to first order,
\[
 \qchain\simeq\frac12+\frac{\delta}{2\sigma}\,\mathbb E M_p.
\]
Expanding the logit around $1/2$ gives
\[
 \logit\qchain\simeq\frac{2\delta}{\sigma}\,\mathbb E M_p.
\]
With one pattern, $M_1=|Z|$. Dividing by this one-pattern response, with
the same equilibrium-log-odds reference, gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification}.
The calculation averages sign probabilities before taking log-odds.
It gives sign odds given the winning pattern, not all pattern-pair frequencies.

\paragraph{Two patterns.}
For $p=2$, the largest miss in Table~\ref{tab:transfer}, setting $y_i=\xi_i^1x_i$
splits the model exactly into two non-interacting CW blocks of $980$--$1068$
spins, whose signs agree exactly when pattern 1 wins.
Treating the blocks as independent and equal doubles the logit $0.343384$ of the
one-pattern runs at $N=1024$; dividing by the exact CW $\logit\qeq=3.575$ at $N=2048$
gives $c=0.19212$, against the measured $0.18783$ $[0.184,0.191]$ and the Gaussian
prediction $0.169$.
The block sizes and initialization remain unmatched.

\paragraph{Pattern correlations.}
For $p$ patterns,
$C_{\mu\nu}=N^{-1}\sum_i\xi_i^\mu\xi_i^\nu$ records their pairwise
correlations. For continuous-time random-site dynamics at zero field,
averaging the expected Gibbs redraw over sites, weighted by $\xi_i^\mu$, gives
\[
 \dot m^\mu\simeq-m^\mu+
 \frac1N\sum_i\xi_i^\mu\tanh\!\left(\beta\sum_\nu\xi_i^\nu m^\nu\right)
 \simeq-m^\mu+\beta\sum_\nu C_{\mu\nu}m^\nu.
\]
The first approximation neglects the self-exclusion correction of order
$p/N$; the second uses $\tanh z\simeq z$ for small overlaps.
The drift of $\boldsymbol m=(m^1,\ldots,m^p)$ is thus $(\beta C-I)\boldsymbol m$.
Along an eigenvector of $C$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, an overlap grows
at rate $\beta\lambda-1$, beginning at $\beta=1/\lambda$ rather than
at $\beta=1$ as for $C=I$.
Each off-diagonal entry averages $N$ independent random signs, so its
standard deviation is $N^{-1/2}$. For fixed $p$, the eigenvalue shifts and
the resulting transition shifts have this order, comparable to the
critical window of Section~\ref{sec:limits}.
Separating these shifts from the selection range $\gamma T\sim\sqrt\gamma$ would
require $N\gamma\gg1$. This comparison does not identify the cause of the
measured size trend.
