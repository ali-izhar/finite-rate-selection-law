\begin{abstract}
A sampler can generate plausible examples in the wrong proportions. We show
how this happens in annealed Gibbs sampling, which updates one variable at a
time while lowering the temperature. In the Curie--Weiss model, binary
variables tend to share one sign, giving two modes, and a weak field makes one
mode more probable. Each run commits to a mode near the critical temperature,
while the field still competes with random fluctuations, and rarely switches
afterwards. The samples therefore keep only a fraction of the model's log-odds
between the modes. We turn the classical probability of this commitment, due
to Kondepudi and Nelson, into a law for the fraction that agrees closely with
an exact calculation of the sampler. The law also explains why slower cooling
helps little: on a linear ramp, cooling sixteen times more slowly only doubles
the fraction. Reheating runs that settled away from the favored mode gives them a
second choice, and the exact calculation shows that it again keeps only a fraction. In
a Hopfield model, competition among stored patterns raises this fraction; a
Gaussian approximation of the competition predicts the result to within
11\% across pattern counts and sizes.\footnote{Code and details:
\url{https://github.com/ali-izhar/finite-rate-selection-law}}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Suppose a model of handwritten digits assigns twice as much probability to
``3'' as to ``8''. A sampler should then produce about twice as many ``3''s as
``8''s over many runs. It can produce recognizable examples of both digits and
still get this proportion wrong, and Monte Carlo estimates from its samples are
then biased. How does the sampler change these proportions?

We study annealed Gibbs sampling, which redraws one variable at a time from its
conditional distribution. At high temperature the
distribution is broad; at low temperature its probability concentrates in
modes, regions of likely outcomes separated by unlikely ones. As annealing
lowers the temperature, the barriers between modes grow and the sampler stops mixing between them: later updates mostly sharpen the
mode each run selected and rarely undo the selection \citep{neal2001}.

The sampler can therefore get the mode proportions wrong even when the model
is right. To measure this error alone, we use the Curie--Weiss (CW) model, whose
probabilities, and those of its Gibbs sampler, can be computed exactly. Its binary 
variables tend to align, giving two modes at low temperature: most variables positive, 
or most negative. A weak field favors the positive mode, but near the critical 
temperature it competes with random fluctuations as each run commits to a mode.

We connect a classical prediction of the mode selection
\citep{kondepudinelson1983,kondepudigao1987,nene2018} to this error in mode
proportions. The resulting law gives the fraction of the model's log-odds between the modes
that the samples keep, and shows why slower cooling helps little.
Extended to a Hopfield model, whose modes are stored patterns
and their negatives, the argument predicts that competition among patterns raises this fraction. 
Tested after
reheating, which lets runs that settled away from the favored mode select
again, the prediction closely matches three-pattern runs but misses by up to
$11\%$ at other pattern counts or sizes.

\section{An exact model of mode selection}
\label{sec:model}

The model's state, or \emph{configuration}, is a vector $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)$
of $N$ binary variables $x_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, called spins. The CW model gives
higher probability to configurations whose spins agree:
\begin{equation}
 \pi_{\beta,G}(x)\propto
 \exp\!\left[\frac{\beta}{2(N-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}x_i x_j
              +\beta G\sum_i x_i\right].
 \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
The \emph{inverse temperature} $\beta$ sets how strongly spins prefer to
agree: at $\beta=0$ all configurations are equally likely, and increasing
$\beta$, which is cooling, concentrates probability on aligned
configurations. The \emph{field} $G$, fixed during a run, adds a preference for one sign. 
At fixed $\beta$, the Gibbs sampler converges to the \emph{equilibrium distribution} $\pi_{\beta,G}$.
The \emph{magnetization} $m=N^{-1}\sum_i x_i$ summarizes a configuration:
$m>0$ means mostly positive spins, $m<0$ mostly negative. Without a field, as
$N\to\infty$, the equilibrium $m$ concentrates at $0$ for $\beta<1$ and at two
opposite nonzero values for $\beta>1$. These two groups of configurations are
the two \emph{modes}, and $\beta=1$ marks the \emph{transition}, at the
critical temperature. A positive field makes the positive mode more probable.

A run starts at some $\beta<1$. Each Gibbs update redraws one uniformly chosen
spin; updates occur at random times, $N$ per unit time on average, and this
unit is an \emph{expected sweep}. A cooling schedule raises $\beta$ past the
transition to a final value, by which the run has settled into one mode. Configurations with 
the same number $k$ of positive spins are equally probable and change $k$ at the same 
rates, which lets $k$ alone describe the run: we track $N+1$ probabilities, one for each 
$k=0,\ldots,N$, instead of $2^N$, one for each configuration
\citep{levinluczak2010}. We compute them exactly, without sampling noise, by numerically
solving the linear equations that govern their change (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).

Let $\qchain$ be the probability that the run ends with $m>0$, and $\qeq$ the
probability of $m>0$ under the equilibrium distribution at the final $\beta$, both
conditioned on $m\ne0$. The fraction of the model's log-odds that the samples
keep is
\begin{equation}
 c=\frac{\logit\qchain}{\logit\qeq},
 \qquad \logit q=\log\frac{q}{1-q},
 \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
for $\qeq\ne1/2$: $c=1$ reproduces the equilibrium proportions, and $c=0$
gives equal mode probabilities. If equilibrium odds are $4{:}1$, $c=0.2$
gives sampled odds of $4^{0.2}{:}1\approx1.32{:}1$.

\section{The selection law}
\label{sec:law}

Each run selects its mode in a short window near $\beta=1$. There, a small random imbalance
between positive and negative spins grows into one mode
(Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}a); afterwards, switching between modes takes far
longer than settling within one
\citep{levinluczak2010,dlpcmp2009}. The final proportions are therefore
decided in this window, the only time the field can bias them.

Near the transition, two effects move $m$; Appendix~\ref{app:exact} derives
both. The \emph{drift}, the mean rate of change of $m$, is
$-m+\tanh(\beta m+\beta G)$, approximately \mbox{$(\beta-1)m+G$} near $m=0$
and $\beta=1$. Below $\beta=1$ its first term points against $m$ and pulls an
imbalance back toward zero; above $\beta=1$ it points along $m$ and amplifies
the imbalance. The \emph{noise} comes from the randomness of the Gibbs updates
and adds a variance of about $2/N$ per expected sweep. On a linear ramp at cooling rate $\gamma>0$,
$\beta(t)=1+\gamma t$ crosses $\beta=1$ at $t=0$. The growth rate $\gamma t$
then itself rises with time, and the drift multiplies the imbalance by
$e^{\gamma t^2/2}$, a factor that becomes large after a time $T$ of order
$\gamma^{-1/2}$: this is the window. Within it, the field adds an imbalance of
order $GT$ and the noise adds an imbalance of order $\sqrt{T/N}$ \citep{berglundgentz2002}.
Their ratio, of order $G\sqrt N\,\gamma^{-1/4}$, decides the race: slower
cooling widens the window and favors the field, but only as the fourth root of
the cooling time.

To turn the field-to-noise ratio into the probability that a run ends in the
positive mode, we divide $m$ by its growth factor $e^{\gamma t^2/2}$.
The result, the early \emph{amplitude}, keeps the run's final sign and stops
changing once the window closes. It is the field's push, the same in every run,
plus the accumulated noise, a sum of many small independent Gaussian kicks; in
the linear approximation it is therefore Gaussian across runs, with mean set
by the field and spread by the noise. In units of its spread, it follows $U\sim\mathcal N(a,1)$,
where $a$ is the field-to-noise ratio of the race with its exact prefactor. A
run ends with $m>0$ exactly when $U>0$, which happens with probability
$\Phi(a)$ (the blue area in Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}b), where $\Phi$ is the
standard Gaussian distribution function. For the parameters of Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}b,
$a=0.208$: the noise is about five times stronger than the
field, yet $58\%$ of runs end in the favored mode. This is the classical result
\citep[][Appendix~\ref{app:exact}]{kondepudinelson1983,kondepudigao1987}
\begin{equation}
 q_{\mathrm{sel}}\simeq\Phi(a),\qquad a=G\sqrt N(\pi/\gamma)^{1/4},
 \label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
with $q_{\mathrm{sel}}$ the probability of selecting $m>0$. Without later switching,
$\qchain=q_{\mathrm{sel}}$.

 $N=2048$, $\gamma=0.001$ and $G=0.0006125$.}
 \label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:c} turns $q_{\mathrm{sel}}$ into $c$, giving the \emph{selection
law}. For a weak
field ($|a|\ll1$), the sampled log-odds are $\logit\Phi(a)\approx4a/\sqrt{2\pi}$,
proportional to $a$, hence to $G\sqrt N\,\gamma^{-1/4}$. The equilibrium
log-odds at the final temperature grow instead as $NG$: reversing all $N$
spins leaves the coupling term unchanged and reverses the field term, $\beta GNm$, of order $NG$ in either mode. In their
ratio, the field cancels, and $c$ measures the sampler, not the field's
strength (Appendix~\ref{app:exact} gives the prefactor):
\begin{equation}
 c\propto(N^2\gamma)^{-1/4}.
 \label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
In this regime, cooling sixteen times more slowly doubles $c$; a fourfold
larger $N$ halves it.

The law rests on three assumptions (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}): a slow ramp
($\gamma\ll1$), a start well below $\beta=1$, and little switching after
selection. The noise must also stay small enough for the linear drift to hold,
which $N\gamma\gg1$ guarantees \citep{berglundgentz2002}. The third assumption limits
how slow the cooling can be, since long enough sampling restores the
equilibrium proportions, but a zero-field switch between modes takes on average about
$5\times10^{7}$
expected sweeps already at $N=2048$ and $\beta=1.1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:exact}).

\paragraph{Exact check.}
On linear ramps ($N=1024$--$4096$, $\gamma=0.00025$--$0.004$, $NG$
fixed), exact $c$ exceeds the law by only $0.23$--$1.81\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}a; Table~\ref{tab:linear}),
even
at $N\gamma=0.256$, where $N\gamma\gg1$ fails.

\section{When several modes compete}
\label{sec:test}

CW has two modes. To ask how competition among several modes changes the
fraction $c$, we use the Hopfield model: with $p\ll N$ stored patterns
$\xi^\mu\in\{-1,+1\}^N$ of independent equiprobable entries, its
modes at low temperature are the patterns and their negatives
\citep{ags1985}. A configuration $x$ has probability proportional to
$e^{-\beta E_G(x)}$, with \emph{energy}
\begin{equation}
 E_G(x)=-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i\ne j}\sum_{\mu=1}^p
       \xi_i^\mu\xi_j^\mu x_i x_j-G\sum_i\xi_i^1x_i.
 \label{eq:hopfield}
\end{equation}
The field $G$ favors pattern~1 over its negative. The overlap $m^\mu=N^{-1}\sum_i\xi_i^\mu x_i$ measures
how closely a configuration resembles pattern $\mu$: it is $+1$ at pattern $\mu$ and $-1$ at its negative. Each
sample is labeled $(\mu,\pm)$ by the pattern with the largest $|m^\mu|$ and the
sign of that overlap. Here $\qchain$ and $\qeq$ are positive-sign
probabilities among samples assigned to pattern~1, not pattern frequencies.

Competition raises the fraction. Each run ends in the pattern whose early
overlap is largest in magnitude, and the field biases only the sign of $m^1$.
As in Section~\ref{sec:law}, the early overlap of pattern~1 is a Gaussian
shifted by the field, and at a given magnitude its sign log-odds grow linearly
in that magnitude, because the Gaussian falls ever more steeply away from zero. 
The winner of $p$ competing overlaps has a larger magnitude
than a single overlap does, and its sign is therefore more strongly biased. To
first order in the field, the bias grows by the ratio of the two average
magnitudes:
\begin{equation}
 A_p=\frac{\mathbb E\max_{1\le\mu\le p}|Z_\mu|}{\mathbb E|Z|},
 \qquad c_{\mathrm{pred}}=A_p c_{\mathrm{CW}},
 \label{eq:amplification}
\end{equation}
where $Z$ and the $Z_\mu$ are independent standard Gaussians: $|Z_\mu|$ is the
size of pattern $\mu$'s early overlap in units of its spread. The numerator is
the average size of the winning overlap among $p$ patterns, the denominator
that of a single overlap, as in CW, where the one pattern always wins. Here
$c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ is the exact CW fraction for the Hopfield reheating protocol below, not for a
linear ramp.
Appendix~\ref{app:amplification} derives Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification};
numerical integration gives $A_1=1$, $A_2=\sqrt2$,
$A_3=1.662$, and $A_4=1.836$. The prediction assumes independent Gaussian early overlaps with
a common variance, a field shifting
only the mean of $m^1$, a final pattern
decided by the largest $|m^\mu|$, and equal Hopfield and CW equilibrium sign
preferences. Even for $p\ll N$, neither the independence nor
this equality is guaranteed.

\paragraph{Reheating after a filtered start.}
We test the prediction where it matters for a sampler: on runs that
settled away from the favored mode and are reheated to select again. Hopfield runs use \emph{permutation sweeps}, redrawing every spin once in fresh random order ($N$ updates, like an
expected sweep), and cool through a \emph{schedule} from $\beta=0.6$ to $1.6$, set at $N=2048$ and reused at
every size (Table~\ref{tab:schedule}).

Each run has three stages: a brief cooling at two
sweeps per temperature, after which we remove the runs
that ended in the favored mode, $(1,+)$, leaving a \emph{filtered
start}; a \emph{reheat} of $30$ sweeps at $\beta=0.7$, where the drift pulls
imbalances back toward zero; and a \emph{final cooling} through the schedule, in which the runs select again. The same $(1,+)$ removal applies at $G<0$; a memory of it would
break the spin-flip symmetry $\qchain(G)+\qchain(-G)=1$. The measured sums are
consistent with $1$ (Table~\ref{tab:counts}), which detects no memory but does not show that
reheated runs behave like freshly cooled ones. For this
protocol, exact CW calculation gives the $c_{\mathrm{CW}}=0.120$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification} at $N=2048$; sixteenfold longer final
cooling raises it only $1.48$-fold, not the twofold of Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling}
(Table~\ref{tab:schedule}).

\paragraph{Result.}
For three patterns at $N=2048$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification} predicts
$c_{\mathrm{pred}}=A_3c_{\mathrm{CW}}=1.662\times0.120=0.199$, without any
Hopfield samples. Unlike
CW's, the Hopfield equilibrium log-odds are estimated from the mean overlap
in separate zero-field runs, by the spin-reversal argument of Section~\ref{sec:law}
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

If $c$ is a property of the sampler, sampled and equilibrium log-odds are
proportional as the field varies, with slope $c$; the eight nonzero fields of the \emph{field grid}
approximately show this (Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b). Slopes for twenty independent
pattern sets, weighted by their pattern-1 samples, average to $c=0.199$ ($95\%$ interval $[0.1908,0.2095]$),
matching the prediction. Equal pattern-set weights give $0.2101$, just above
the interval (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). The agreement may partly reflect
cancelling errors (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Where the prediction departs}
\label{sec:limits}

The three-pattern match does not hold everywhere: across pattern counts and
sizes, the prediction misses by up to $11\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:transfer};
Appendix~\ref{app:amplification} treats $p=2$, the largest miss, by an exact
decomposition). For $p=3$, the
single-field estimate of Table~\ref{tab:transfer}, $0.201$, agrees with the field-grid fit, $0.199$.

With one
pattern there is no competition ($A_1=1$), and the Hopfield runs should
reproduce $c_{\mathrm{CW}}$; instead, separate, larger one-pattern runs give
$1.063\,c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ at $N=2048$ (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). The two
samplers differ in update order (permutation versus expected sweeps) and
interaction strength ($1/N$ versus $1/(N-1)$), and the prediction ignores both.
Applying the same factor at $p=3$ gives $0.212$, outside the field-grid
interval; the three-pattern match would then need an offsetting error of
similar size.

The size dependence also departs.
Measured $c$ falls as $N^{-0.36}$ over $N=1024$--$4096$, the prediction as
$N^{-0.46}$ (two-point slopes, without uncertainties; Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling}
gives $N^{-1/2}$ for a fixed ramp), and the two cross near $N=2048$, where the
schedule was set. Scaling the prediction by the one-pattern
ratio at each size does not remove this trend (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). These fixed-schedule comparisons do not measure how Hopfield sampling
depends on cooling rate.

Pattern correlations are one untested explanation of the size trend: random
patterns have correlations of order $N^{-1/2}$, which shift the $\beta$ at which
the overlaps begin to grow by as much as the \emph{critical window}, the range of
$\beta$ over which fluctuations blur the transition at finite $N$
(Appendix~\ref{app:amplification}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The selection probability is classical
\citep{kondepudinelson1983,kondepudigao1987}; the paper turns it into the
fraction $c$ of the model's log-odds that the samples keep, checks it against the exact CW chain, and extends it to competing
modes. Cooling studies of Ising chains \citep{suzuki2009} measure a different
error, misaligned regions and excess energy, rather than mode probabilities.

Annealed importance sampling weights annealed runs instead of reselecting
them, to estimate equilibrium averages \citep{neal2001}; comparing it with reheating at equal cost
would need valid weights for the filtered start and an assessment of estimator
bias and variance. Diffusion models with an exact
score reverse a noising process without equilibrating at each step
\citep{biroli2024}; extending the calculation here needs their reverse dynamics
and discrete updates. In diffusion models, a variance-preserving noise schedule can miss mode asymmetry, while suitable
schedules recover mode probabilities in Gaussian mixtures and CW
\citep{aranguri2025}.

The exact CW calculation scores any cooling schedule by its error in mode
probabilities, without sampling noise; applying $c$ to other models requires
their equilibrium log-odds. Measuring Hopfield overlaps during cooling could
show whether pattern correlations explain the remaining discrepancies.

\section{Selection formula and exact-chain calculations}
\label{app:exact}

This appendix derives the selection law of Section~\ref{sec:law}, states when
it holds, and describes the exact calculation that tests it.

\paragraph{From spin updates to drift and noise.}
Section~\ref{sec:law} describes $m$ by a drift and a noise, and both follow from
single-spin updates. For a chosen spin, let $\ell_i=\beta(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j\ne i}x_j+h$,
where $h=\beta G$. Holding the other spins fixed, Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}
assigns weights $e^{\ell_i}$ and $e^{-\ell_i}$ to its two values, so the
redrawn spin has mean $\tanh\ell_i$. For large $N$, the neighbors' average is approximately $m$.
A uniformly chosen spin has old mean $m$, so one update changes $m$ by
$[\tanh(\beta m+h)-m]/N$ in expectation. With $N$ updates per expected sweep,
the drift is $\dot m=-m+\tanh(\beta m+h)$.
Near $m=0$ and in a weak field, an update flips a spin with probability about
one half, and each flip changes $m$ by $2/N$. The noise variance per expected sweep
is therefore approximately $N\times\tfrac12\times(2/N)^2=2/N$.
A short interval contains many updates that change $m$ little; we
approximate the centered sum of their jumps by a Gaussian.
Expanding $\tanh z=z-z^3/3+\cdots$ near $\beta=1$, retaining the field,
linear, and cubic terms, and writing $G$ for $h=\beta G$ (equal at $\beta=1$) gives
\begin{equation}
 dm=\big[(\beta-1)m-m^3/3+G\big]dt+\sqrt{2/N}\,dW_t,
 \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion.
At $G=0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:sde} is the swept pitchfork bifurcation analyzed by
\citet{berglundgentz2002}: one stable value becomes two.

\paragraph{Removing growth without changing the sign.}
On a linear ramp, $\beta-1=\gamma t$, the growth of $m$ can be divided out
without changing its sign. While $m$ is small, omit the cubic term. Without field or noise, $\dot m=\gamma t m$ has the positive growth
factor $e^{\gamma t^2/2}$. Set $Y_t=e^{-\gamma t^2/2}m_t$ and start at
$t_0<0$. The product rule gives
\[
 Y_t=e^{-\gamma t_0^2/2}m_{t_0}
       +G\int_{t_0}^t e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,du
       +\sqrt{2/N}\int_{t_0}^t e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,dW_u.
\]
The three terms are the remaining initial imbalance, the accumulated field,
and the accumulated noise. With $w(u)=e^{-\gamma u^2/2}$, the noise integral
is the limit of sums $\sum_j w(u_j)\sqrt{\Delta u}\,\epsilon_j$ of independent
standard Gaussians $\epsilon_j$: a Gaussian with mean zero and variance
$\sum_j w(u_j)^2\Delta u$. The field term therefore uses $w$, and the noise
variance uses $w^2$.

The weights are appreciable over times of order $\gamma^{-1/2}$ around
the transition. An early start suppresses the initial term; a late end
lets both integrals settle. Extending their limits to the whole real line
gives the amplitude $Y$ with
\begin{align*}
 \mu_Y=\mathbb E Y
 &=G\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u^2/2}\,du
   =G\sqrt{2\pi/\gamma},\\
 V_Y=\operatorname{Var}(Y)
 &=\frac2N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma u^2}\,du
   =\frac2N\sqrt{\pi/\gamma}.
\end{align*}
Hence $U=Y/\sqrt{V_Y}=a+Z$, where $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and
$a=\mu_Y/\sqrt{V_Y}=G\sqrt N(\pi/\gamma)^{1/4}$.
The positive mode is selected when $U>0$, with probability
$\Pr(Z>-a)=\Phi(a)$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}.
These infinite limits approximate a finite cooling interval; the integrals must settle before the cubic term
substantially changes the amplitude.

\paragraph{From a sign probability to retained log-odds.}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:c} then turns $\Phi(a)$ into $c$. For small $a$, $\Phi(a)=1/2+a/\sqrt{2\pi}+O(a^3)$.
The derivative of $\logit q$ at $q=1/2$ is $4$, so
$\logit\Phi(a)=4a/\sqrt{2\pi}+O(a^3)$.
At the final inverse temperature $\beta_f$, a field adds $\beta_f NGm$
to each configuration's log weight. To first order in $G$, the positive
mode's log mass changes by its average, $\beta_f NG\bar m_f$, and the
negative mode's by the opposite amount. Here $\bar m_f$ is the zero-field
mean magnetization in the positive mode at $\beta_f$.
The equilibrium log-odds is thus approximately $2\beta_f NG\bar m_f$.
Dividing the two leading log-odds gives
\[
 c\simeq\frac{\sqrt2\,\pi^{-1/4}}{\beta_f\bar m_f}
             (N^2\gamma)^{-1/4}.
\]

\paragraph{Regime.}
With $a$ bounded, the linear approximation and the separation from the critical
window both follow from $N\gamma\gg1$. Over the selection
time $T\sim\gamma^{-1/2}$, the linear coefficient $\gamma T$ is of order
$\sqrt\gamma$, so the cubic term matters once $|m|\sim\gamma^{1/4}$; the noise
accumulated over $T$, of order $\sqrt{T/N}\sim N^{-1/2}\gamma^{-1/4}$, is a
fraction $(N\gamma)^{-1/2}$ of this scale, and the field's contribution, a fraction
$|G|\gamma^{-3/4}=\pi^{-1/4}a(N\gamma)^{-1/2}$ of this scale, is small too when $a$ stays bounded. Selection must also
span more than the critical window: to leading order in $N$, Stirling's
formula and the interaction $N\beta m^2/2$ give
$\log\pi(m)\simeq\mathrm{constant}+N[(\beta-1)m^2/2-m^4/12]$, so at $\beta=1$
fluctuations reach $|m|\sim N^{-1/4}$ and the quadratic term matters when
$|\beta-1|\sim N^{-1/2}$, the critical window of Section~\ref{sec:limits}. The
selection range $\gamma T\sim\sqrt\gamma$ exceeds it when $N\gamma\gg1$. These
conditions allow linear selection before nonlinear growth fixes the magnitude;
they do not exclude later switching.

\paragraph{Exact reduction and numerical evolution.}
To test the law, we compute the sampler's distribution exactly. Waiting times between CW updates are independent exponentials with mean
$1/N$, so the count $k$ of positive spins is a Markov chain whose rates, at
a given temperature, depend only on $k$. Each spin updates once per expected
sweep on average, so each rate is the number of eligible spins ($N-k$
negative or $k$ positive) times their redraw probability:
\begin{align}
 W_k^+&=\frac{N-k}{2}\left[1+\tanh\!\left(\beta\frac{2k-N+1}{N-1}+h\right)\right],\\
 W_k^-&=\frac{k}{2}\left[1-\tanh\!\left(\beta\frac{2k-N-1}{N-1}+h\right)\right],
 \label{eq:rates}
\end{align}
where $h=\beta G$; the $+1$ and $-1$ exclude the updated spin from the
average of its neighbors. Equal probability flow between adjacent counts,
$\pi_kW_k^+=\pi_{k+1}W_{k+1}^-$ (\emph{detailed balance}), holds for
\begin{equation}
 \pi_k\propto\binom Nk
 \exp\!\left[\frac{\beta}{2(N-1)}\big((2k-N)^2-N\big)+h(2k-N)\right],
 \label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
where the binomial factor counts configurations with the same $k$.
The conditional reference is
$\qeq=\sum_{k>N/2}\pi_k/\sum_{k\ne N/2}\pi_k$;
the omitted zero-magnetization mass is below $10^{-36}$ at $\beta_f=1.6$ for
$N\ge512$.
We evolve the count probabilities $P_k(t)$,
\[
 \dot P_k=W_{k-1}^+P_{k-1}+W_{k+1}^-P_{k+1}-(W_k^++W_k^-)P_k,
\]
by implicit Euler steps with Richardson extrapolation. The result therefore
has no sampling error.

\paragraph{Linear ramps.}
The ramps of Section~\ref{sec:law} start in the equilibrium distribution at
$\beta=0.7$ and cool to $\beta=1.6$, with $G=0.0006125\times2048/N$; a fixed $NG$
keeps the equilibrium log-odds nearly constant across sizes (time steps:
Table~\ref{tab:runs}). Table~\ref{tab:linear} compares the
resulting $c_{\mathrm{num}}$ with the full-formula prediction
$c_{\mathrm{sel}}=\logit\Phi(a)/\logit\qeq$, with $a$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}.
The excess over $c_{\mathrm{sel}}$ halves when $N$ doubles at
$\gamma=0.004$ and $0.001$, but not at $0.00025$.
We did not derive the correction that causes this excess.

\paragraph{Fixed schedule.}
The Hopfield prediction needs $c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ for the protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:test}, computed the same way. Mirroring that protocol, the CW
evolution starts with
$\operatorname{Binomial}(N,1/2)$ counts, runs two sweeps at each temperature of
Table~\ref{tab:schedule}, removes $k\ge N/2$ (including $m=0$) and renormalizes,
reheats for $30$ sweeps at $\beta=0.7$, then applies Table~\ref{tab:schedule}.
The removal
keeps $k<N/2$ for both field signs, as the Hopfield runs remove $(1,+)$ for
both, so the two logits in $c_{\mathrm{CW}}$ share the sign of $G$.
Switching after selection is unlikely while each dwell is much shorter than the
mean time for the exact zero-field chain to pass between mode centers: at
$N=2048$, about $10^{7.7}$, $10^{22.9}$, and $10^{137.8}$ expected
sweeps at $\beta=1.1$, $1.2$, and $1.6$, against $834$ sweeps in the whole schedule.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling} describes a constant-rate ramp from a start well below
$\beta=1$, whereas this schedule dwells at ten fixed temperatures after a
filtered start and reheating; we did not isolate which difference makes
sixteenfold final cooling raise $c$ less than twofold (Table~\ref{tab:schedule}).

\paragraph{Schedule design.}
The dwells of that schedule come from relaxation times. The relaxation time $\tau$ is the inverse \emph{spectral gap}, the smallest
positive decay rate of the chain at fixed temperature: in time $\tau$, the
slowest deviation from equilibrium shrinks by $e^{-1}$. The six-$\tau$ dwell of
Table~\ref{tab:schedule} is a relaxation prescription, not a bound on the error
in final mode probabilities. To measure relaxation within a sign, the
\emph{reflected} chain (even $N$) keeps $k=N/2,\ldots,N$, sets $W^-_{N/2}=0$,
and leaves the other rates unchanged. At and below the transition, the schedule uses the full
chain, because reflection would remove the slow relaxation of the balance between signs.
At $\beta=1$ and $G=0$, the full-chain inverse gap is $42.2$, $59.0$, and $82.7$ sweeps
for $N=1024$, $2048$, and $4096$; it grows as $\sqrt N$, so dwells redesigned for each $N$ would too.

\section{Simulation and statistical methods}
\label{app:protocol}

This appendix describes how the Hopfield measurements of Sections~\ref{sec:test}
and~\ref{sec:limits} are made and how their uncertainty is estimated.

\paragraph{Sampling and labels.}
The Hopfield runs use Gibbs updates for the energy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hopfield}: each update
redraws $x_i$ with probability
$\Pr(x_i=+1\mid x_{-i})=[1+\tanh(\beta H_i)]/2$, where $x_{-i}$ denotes the other spins and
$H_i=N^{-1}\sum_\mu\xi_i^\mu\sum_j\xi_j^\mu x_j-(p/N)x_i+G\xi_i^1$;
the term $-(p/N)x_i$ removes self-interaction. All runs begin with
independent unbiased spins, and the field is present during the
initial cooling, reheating, and final cooling. Only final samples labeled
pattern 1 with nonzero sign (\emph{retained} samples) enter the estimate.
Every nonzero-field point's sample interval lies strictly between $1/2$ and its reference
interval; all eight nonzero fields of Table~\ref{tab:counts} enter the fit, and
none was excluded.
Table~\ref{tab:runs} lists seeds and run counts.

\paragraph{Equilibrium reference.}
For each pattern set $s$, $256$ runs start at $+\xi^1$ and run at
$\beta_f=1.6$, $G=0$. After $60$ equilibration sweeps, overlaps are recorded
for $200$ sweeps; all measurements were labeled $(1,+)$.
Their mean $\bar m^1_s$ gives the weak-field reference
$\logit q_{\mathrm{eq},s}(G)\approx2\beta_f NG\bar m^1_s$.
The standard error (SE) divides the standard deviation of the pattern set's
$256\times200$ overlaps by
$\sqrt{256\times200/\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}}$, with $\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}=1.6946$ the reflected CW
relaxation time at $N=2048$, $\beta=1.6$, $G=0$.
$\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is an approximate correlation correction, fixed before the runs,
not a measured Hopfield correlation time.
The standard error of the $256$ independent run means, which needs no correlation time,
is $0.85$--$1.05$ times the reported SE for each pattern set.
The log-odds envelope adds the statistical term $1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$ and a
$1\%$ systematic allowance for the reference, a design choice rather than a
measured bound (a common $1\%$ increase in the overlaps reduces $c$ by about
$1\%$):
\[
 \Delta L=2\beta_f N|G|\big[1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(\bar m^1)+0.01\bar m^1\big].
\]
The pooled references in Table~\ref{tab:counts} and
Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b weight pattern set $s$ by $\omega_s=n_s(G)/\sum_r n_r(G)$,
with $n_s(G)$ its retained samples at field $G$:
$\bar m^1=\sum_s \omega_s\bar m^1_s$ and
$\mathrm{SE}(\bar m^1)^2=\sum_s \omega_s^2\mathrm{SE}(\bar m^1_s)^2$.
In Figure~\ref{fig:validation}b, this envelope (both terms) gives the horizontal error bars, and pooled
$95\%$ Wilson intervals give the vertical ones.
The bootstrap excludes the allowance and
error in the Hopfield equilibrium approximation.
The weak-field formula itself is accurate: for CW at $N=2048$ and
$\beta=1.6$, it differs from the exact $\qeq$ by at most $2.108\times10^{-8}$ over the
eight fields.

\paragraph{Slope estimate.}
With this reference, each pattern set gives a slope, and the slopes are
averaged. For field $j$ and pattern set $s$, let $x_{js}=\logit q_{\mathrm{eq},js}$,
$y_{js}=\logit\widehat q_{js}$ with $\widehat q_{js}=n_{+,js}/n_{js}$, and
$[L_{js},U_{js}]$ the $95\%$ Wilson interval of $\widehat q_{js}$ \citep{wilson1927}.
Each pattern set's slope weights its measurements by the inverse squared logit half-width
of this interval:
\[
 w_{js}=\left[\frac{\logit U_{js}-\logit L_{js}}{2\times1.96}\right]^{-2},
 \qquad c_s=\frac{\sum_j w_{js}x_{js}y_{js}}{\sum_j w_{js}x_{js}^2}.
\]
The grid estimate is $\widehat c_{\mathrm{grid}}=\sum_s n_sc_s/\sum_s n_s$,
with $n_s$ the retained samples over the set's eight fields.
Resampling the twenty pattern sets $20{,}000$ times, with reference means $\bar m^1_s$ held fixed,
gives $\widehat c_{\mathrm{grid}}=0.1994$ and the
$2.5$th--$97.5$th percentile interval $[0.1908,0.2095]$.
Three alternative estimators give $0.1994$--$0.1997$: weights
$n_{js}\tilde q_{js}(1-\tilde q_{js})$, with $\tilde q_{js}$ the Wilson-interval center;
all fields pooled before fitting; and a pooled binomial logistic fit
$\logit q=\alpha+cx$ (intercept $0.000944$).

\paragraph{Pattern-count and size comparisons.}
Section~\ref{sec:limits} compares settings with fewer runs, at a single field
$\pm G$ and without a Hopfield reference. With $n_{+,s}(G)$ and $n_s(G)$ the positive and total retained counts of
pattern set $s$ at field $G$, let
\[
 S_s=n_{+,s}(+G)+n_s(-G)-n_{+,s}(-G),\qquad
 R_s=n_s(+G)+n_s(-G).
\]
The pooled estimator, which assumes $\qchain(G)+\qchain(-G)=1$
(checked only for $p=3$, $N=2048$; Table~\ref{tab:counts}),
\[
 \widehat c_{\mathrm{scan}}=\logit\Big(\sum_s S_s\Big/\sum_s R_s\Big)\Big/\logit q_{\mathrm{eq}}^{\mathrm{CW}}(N),
\]
uses the CW reference in place of the Hopfield one and does not fit a slope
per pattern set. The $p=3$, $N=2048$ row of Table~\ref{tab:transfer} comes from separate runs on
twelve of the field grid's pattern sets ($s=3$--$14$).
One-pattern runs ($s=0$; full protocol with the filtered start; $|G|=0.0006125$)
give $c$ equal to $1.065$, $1.126$, $1.063$, and $1.020$ times
$c_{\mathrm{CW}}=0.227$, $0.171$, $0.120$, $0.090$ at $N=512$, $1024$, $2048$, $4096$.
Scaled by these ratios, the $p=3$ prediction becomes $0.319$ at $N=1024$ and
$0.153$ at $N=4096$; measured $c$ remains $18\%$ below and $4\%$ above it

\section{Amplification and pattern correlations}
\label{app:amplification}

This appendix derives the gain $A_p$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification} and examines two
weak points of the prediction: two patterns and pattern correlations.

\paragraph{Conditioning on the winning pattern.}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification} follows from conditioning on the pattern that
wins. Under the independent-Gaussian approximation, let
$m^1\sim\mathcal N(\delta,\sigma^2)$, where $\delta$ is the field-induced
mean shift and the other overlaps have zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$.
The density $f$ of $m^1$ gives $f(r)/f(-r)=\exp(2r\delta/\sigma^2)$ at
magnitude $r>0$.
Winning depends only on the magnitude, so it does not change these sign
odds at fixed $r$:
\[
 \Pr(m^1>0\mid |m^1|=r,\ \text{pattern 1 wins})
 =\frac{1}{1+e^{-2r\delta/\sigma^2}}
 \simeq\frac12+\frac{r\delta}{2\sigma^2}.
\]
At $\delta=0$, all patterns have the same distribution, so the
winning magnitude is distributed as $\sigma M_p$, where
$M_p=\max_{1\le\mu\le p}|Z_\mu|$ for independent standard Gaussians $Z_\mu$.
The magnitude distribution is even in $\delta$, so its first-order change is zero.
Averaging the sign probability over winning magnitudes then gives, to first order,
\[
 \qchain\simeq\frac12+\frac{\delta}{2\sigma}\,\mathbb E M_p.
\]
Expanding the logit around $1/2$ gives
\[
 \logit\qchain\simeq\frac{2\delta}{\sigma}\,\mathbb E M_p.
\]
With one pattern, $M_1=|Z|$. Dividing by this one-pattern response, with
the same equilibrium-log-odds reference, gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:amplification}.
The calculation averages sign probabilities before taking log-odds.
It gives sign odds given the winning pattern, not all pattern-pair frequencies.

\paragraph{Two patterns.}
For $p=2$, the largest miss in Table~\ref{tab:transfer}, an exact decomposition
gives a sharper estimate. With $y_i=\xi_i^1x_i$, the spins on which the two
patterns agree and those on which they disagree form two non-interacting CW
blocks, of $980$--$1068$ spins in our pattern sets. Pattern~1 wins exactly when
the two blocks take the same sign, and their odds then multiply. Treating the
blocks as independent and equal, each a CW model of about $N/2=1024$ spins,
therefore doubles the logit $0.343384$ of the one-pattern runs at $N=1024$.
Dividing by the exact CW $\logit\qeq=3.575$ at $N=2048$ gives $c=0.19212$,
against the measured $0.18783$ $[0.184,0.191]$ and the Gaussian prediction
$0.169$. The block sizes and initialization remain unmatched.

\paragraph{Pattern correlations.}
Section~\ref{sec:limits} names pattern correlations as one possible cause of
the size trend; this paragraph shows that the transition shifts they cause stay comparable to
the critical window as $N$ grows. For $p$ patterns,
$C_{\mu\nu}=N^{-1}\sum_i\xi_i^\mu\xi_i^\nu$ records their pairwise
correlations. For continuous-time random-site dynamics at zero field,
averaging the expected Gibbs redraw over sites, weighted by $\xi_i^\mu$, gives
\[
 \dot m^\mu\simeq-m^\mu+
 \frac1N\sum_i\xi_i^\mu\tanh\!\left(\beta\sum_\nu\xi_i^\nu m^\nu\right)
 \simeq-m^\mu+\beta\sum_\nu C_{\mu\nu}m^\nu.
\]
The first approximation neglects the self-exclusion correction of order
$p/N$; the second uses $\tanh z\simeq z$ for small overlaps.
The drift of $\boldsymbol m=(m^1,\ldots,m^p)$ is thus $(\beta C-I)\boldsymbol m$.
Along an eigenvector of $C$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, an overlap grows
at rate $\beta\lambda-1$, beginning at $\beta=1/\lambda$ rather than
at $\beta=1$ as for $C=I$.
Each off-diagonal entry averages $N$ independent random signs, so its
standard deviation is $N^{-1/2}$. For fixed $p$, the eigenvalue shifts and
the resulting transition shifts have this order, comparable to the
critical window of Section~\ref{sec:limits}.
Separating these shifts from the selection range $\gamma T\sim\sqrt\gamma$ would
require $N\gamma\gg1$. This comparison does not identify the cause of the
measured size trend.
