A Finite-Rate Selection Law for Annealed Gibbs Sampling

A sampler can produce clear samples in the wrong proportions. Cooled at a finite rate, a Gibbs sampler can turn the model’s 36 : 1 preference between two modes into 1.27 : 1.

When does a run choose its mode?

Cooling raises β, the way a lower temperature sharpens a softmax. Near β = 1 one valley splits into two modes, A and B.

live · Curie–Weiss model, N = 1,024 variables

Result 1A run picks its mode near β = 1 and keeps it: at N = 2,048 a switch takes about 5 × 107 sweeps, and the longest ramp lasts 3,600.

How much of the model’s preference survives?

A weak field G makes the model prefer mode A. The sampler keeps a fraction c of that preference, measured in log-odds:

exact Curie–Weiss chain · N = 2,048 · the test’s ramp

Result 2The sampler keeps cCW = 0.0659 of the log-odds, and this fraction barely changes with the weak field.

Where does the rest of the preference go?

Each line is one run on the test’s ramp, cooling through β = 1 with the same weak field toward A. A sweep is N updates, about one per variable.

live · drift-plus-noise model of the mean spin m

Result 3The sign is set in the shaded window, where the field’s push is small next to the noise. It only tilts a coin: P(A) = Φ(push ÷ spread), with Φ the Gaussian CDF (Kondepudi and Nelson 1983).

Assumes a slow ramp from well below β = 1, a nearly linear drift, and little later switching.

Does sampling more slowly fix it?

A slower ramp gives the field more time, and the fluctuations too.

exact Curie–Weiss chain · linear ramps

Result 4On a linear ramp c ∝ (N²γ)−1/4: 16× the sweeps only doubles c, and 4× the model size halves it.

Weak field only (G√N(π/γ)1/4 ≪ 1); very slow cooling at fixed N recovers equilibrium.

What changes when patterns compete?

A Hopfield network stores p patterns, and the field favours pattern 1’s + sign over its − sign. c compares the sign log-odds of the runs that end in pattern 1, not how often pattern 1 wins. You might expect competition to lose more of the preference.

live · Gaussian draws

Result 5It keeps more: a larger early overlap carries more of the field, so the prediction multiplies c by Ap = kept mean ÷ all mean.

Assumed: the early overlaps are independent Gaussians of common variance, the field shifts only overlap 1, and the largest early overlap decides the final pattern.

Where the prediction falls short

  1. Four patterns measure 6% low, at the edge of significance. Their 95% interval is [0.884, 1.00]; its upper end is 0.997–1.002 over 200 bootstrap seeds. All four competition rows lie 0.5–6% below their predictions.
  2. Random patterns are correlated. The prediction assumes independent overlaps of common variance, which needs Nγ ≫ p; here p/(Nγ) is 0.18 to 0.73. The protocol reads deficits ordered by p/(Nγ) as a finite-rate correction and unordered ones as a failed assumption. They are not ordered, although their differences lie within the intervals, so Ap is not supported as derived: the largest deficit is at p = 4, N = 2,048, not at p = 3, N = 1,024, where p/(Nγ) is largest.
  3. Single pattern sets depart from the prediction (post-hoc analysis). The pooled agreement is an average over pattern sets, not a property of one network: pattern 1 should win a share 1/p of the runs in every set; over the four-pattern sets it wins 0.09 to 0.71. Per-set c falls as this share rises and, beyond sampling noise, varies between sets with a standard deviation of 7–13% of the prediction. The pooled ratio counts each set by its pattern-1 runs, so it weights most the sets with a high win share and low c; the mean of the per-set ratios is 1.00 for four patterns. Unequal amplitude variances from pattern correlations would produce the uneven shares and the falling c (untested).
  4. One cooling rate, one field. The test uses one cooling rate and one field strength (NG), so it does not show how competition depends on the cooling rate.
  5. Single-spin Gibbs sampling only. Other samplers, such as diffusion models (Biroli et al. 2024), need their own calculation.
