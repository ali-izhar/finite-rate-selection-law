A Finite-Rate Selection Law for Annealed Gibbs Sampling

A sampler can produce clear samples in the wrong proportions. In annealed Gibbs sampling, a weak field competes with random fluctuations while cooling picks a mode.

WhyWhy is the preference lost?

The runs choose a mode near β = 1, where the field’s push toward A is still weak next to random fluctuations. After the modes form, switching can take much longer than settling within one (paper Sec. 3).

The dashed mark on the c meter is the selection formula (Eq. 3) for this field, size and cooling rate, divided by the model’s own log-odds; nothing is fitted. Try more sweeps per run: slower cooling helps, but less than you might expect (chapter 5).

The formula and the headline’s 0.199 are linked by one step. On linear ramps the formula matches the exact CW chain within 1.81%, with the chain slightly above it in all nine cases (Table 2). On the paper’s cooling schedule, that chain gives cCW = 0.120, and the amplification factor A3 = 1.662 for three patterns turns it into 0.199 (chapter 6).

CaveatsIs this the paper’s c?

No. Fig. 0 runs a small two-mode model (N = 256, a linear ramp) so that it runs live in your browser, and it has its own c. Its closeness to 0.199 is a coincidence of the settings: c changes with model size and schedule.

The headline above is the paper’s test (chapter 7): three stored patterns at N = 2,048, predicted 0.120 × 1.662 = 0.199 (nothing fitted to the network runs), measured 0.199 (95%: 0.1908–0.2095). Besides this test, p = 4 also falls inside its interval; the other settings miss by up to 11% (Limits 1).

    Chapter 1 · β 0.80

    When does a run choose its mode?

    Near β = 1 one mode splits into two, and a small random imbalance grows into one of them. Switching modes afterwards can take far longer than settling in one.

    β is the inverse temperature, like softmax sharpness: small β is broad, large β concentrated. Cooling raises β; the modes form at β = 1.
    Fig. 1 live · N = 1,024, as in paper Fig. 1a

    Result 1Near β = 1 one valley splits in two, and the sign a run picks there can decide its final mode.

    WhyWhen are the proportions decided?

    Near β = 1 a small random imbalance between + and − spins can grow into either mode, and the field biases this early choice. Once the modes have formed, switching between them can take much longer than settling within one (Levin, Luczak and Peres 2010; Ding, Lubetzky and Peres 2009), so the sign chosen early can decide the final proportions.

    The rest of the page asks how much of a small preference survives that choice.

    The mathThe model, and the drift that splits it

    The paper uses the simplest model with two modes, the Curie–Weiss model, whose probabilities and sampler both reduce exactly to the count of + spins (Sec. 2): N binary spins that all pull toward agreeing with each other,

    G is the field, fixed during a cooling run. Mode A is “mostly +1”, mode B is “mostly −1”, and the mean spin, the magnetisation m, says which one a sample is in. For large N the magnetisation approximately follows the drift (App. A)

    With no field, for β < 1 only m = 0 is stable; for β > 1 two stable states appear at opposite nonzero m.

    Chapter 2 · side note

    Why does the schedule linger at β = 1?

    Relaxation is slowest near β = 1, so the schedule, six relaxation times per temperature, spends 355 of 834 sweeps there.

    Fig. 2 paper Table 3 · relaxation times

    Result 2The relaxation-time peak near β = 1 grows as √N; the schedule is set at N = 2,048.

    WhyHow is each dwell chosen?

    The Gibbs sampler redraws one spin at a time. At the transition the landscape over m has a flat bottom: the linear pull back to equilibrium is zero, and only the weak cubic pull m³/3 remains. Physicists call this critical slowing down. This chapter is a side note: you can skip to chapter 3.

    The relaxation time τ is the inverse spectral gap: the time for the slowest deviation from equilibrium to shrink by a factor e. For β ≤ 1 the schedule uses the full chain; above 1 it uses the chain held on one sign (reflected at m = 0), so τ measures settling within a mode. The dwell at each temperature is ⌈6τ⌉ sweeps, computed at N = 2,048 and reused at every size (Table 3). This is a relaxation rule, not a bound on the error in mode probabilities (App. A).

    Redesigned for each N, a schedule that contains β = 1 would need a √N dwell at that temperature; here it is designed once and held fixed.

    Chapter 3 · β 0.95

    How much of the model’s preference survives?

    Give the model a weak field G favouring mode A (drawn as “3”). The sampler produces A less often than the model says; c measures how much of the preference, in log-odds, survives.

    c is not a constant: it depends on the model size N and on how fast the sampler cools. Every figure states its setting; the Data drawer lists every c on this page.
    Fig. 3 exact CW chain · N = 2,048 · the paper’s protocol

    Result 3On the paper’s protocol the Curie–Weiss sampler keeps cCW = 0.120 of the model’s log-odds (N = 2,048), nearly the same at every field.

    WhyWhy measure the preference in log-odds?

    A small field G pushes every spin toward +1, so the model puts more probability on mode A, the positive mode (m > 0); mode B is the negative one. A perfect sampler would produce mode A exactly as often as the model says. The figure counts what the paper’s sampler produces instead, and turns the gap into one number, the retained share c, measured on the log-odds scale.

    qeq is the model’s probability of mode A at the end of cooling and qchain the share of runs that end there, both among samples with m ≠ 0. For a weak field the sampled and the model’s log-odds are both close to proportional to G (Sec. 3), so G nearly cancels in their ratio c. c = 1 means the sampler reproduces the model; c = 0 is a coin flip. If the model’s odds are 4:1, c = 0.2 gives about 1.32:1 (paper Sec. 2).

    Chapter 4 · β 1.00

    Where does the rest of the preference go?

    It is lost in a short selection interval near β = 1. There the field’s push toward A competes with random fluctuations, and the weak field only tilts the choice.

    Kondepudi and Nelson (1983) and Kondepudi and Gao (1987) derived this sign probability for symmetry-breaking transitions crossed at a finite rate.
    Fig. 4 live · drift-plus-noise equation (Eq. 7)

    Check-point settings (N = 1,024, linear ramp). The paper’s protocol (N = 2,048) gives the Curie–Weiss cCW = 0.120 (Chapter 3).

    Result 4The sign is chosen in an interval of about γ−1/2 sweeps, where the field only tilts a coin tossed by the fluctuations.

    WhyWhy one interval, and not the whole schedule?

    A natural guess is that the loss comes from too few sweeps at each value of β. Before the interval the field has nothing to choose between; after it, switching modes takes much longer than settling, so extra sweeps change little. Only very long sampling at the final temperature recovers the model’s proportions, but at N = 2,048 the zero-field mean time for a mode switch is about 5 × 107 expected sweeps at β = 1.1 (Sec. 3, App. A).

    Inside the interval the weak field competes with the fluctuations, so it tilts a coin rather than deciding it.

    The mathWhere the selection formula comes from

    A classical calculation (Kondepudi and Nelson 1983; Kondepudi and Gao 1987) gives the chance of the positive sign, the selection formula (paper Eq. 3), from three inputs: the field G, the model size N, and the cooling rate γ:

    It equals qchain if later switching is negligible. It assumes a slow ramp starting well below β = 1; Nγ ≫ 1 is a sufficient small-noise condition (Sec. 3). It does not extend to arbitrarily slow cooling at fixed N.

    Near β = 1 the mean spin follows the drift-plus-noise equation (paper Eq. 7), with β = 1 + γt and t in sweeps:

    The growth of m becomes appreciable over about sweeps around β = 1. In that interval the field pushes m by about , while fluctuations of size about per sweep accumulate to about . A run lands on the A side with probability Φ(push ÷ spread), Φ the Gaussian CDF; push ÷ spread is a in (4.1).

    Chapter 5 · β 1.05

    Does sampling more slowly fix it?

    A slower ramp, a smaller rate γ, gives the field more time, and the fluctuations too. How much does it gain?

    Fig. 5a, 5b exact CW chain · paper and repository

    5a’s 1× is a fast 225-sweep ramp, not the paper’s schedule (5b).

    Result 5On a linear ramp, 16× the sweeps only doubles c, c ∝ (N²γ)−1/4; on the paper’s cooling schedule, 16× the final cooling gains only 1.48×.

    WhyWhy does slowing down help so little?

    A slower ramp gives the field more time to push the chain toward the favoured mode, but it also gives the random fluctuations more time to spread. The push grows faster than the spread, but only by the fourth root of the time: 16× the sweeps buys 2× c (the math).

    Fig. 5a checks this on a linear ramp; Fig. 5b runs the paper’s own schedule, which gives the reference cCW = 0.120 that the final test builds on.

    The mathThe scaling law, and where it holds

    The sampler’s log-odds. G is the field per spin toward the positive mode. From the race of chapter 4, the chain’s log-odds grow as the field’s push over the fluctuation spread in the selection interval:

    The model’s log-odds. The two modes differ in log-probability by an amount proportional to N G: a push of G on each of N spins. The chain’s log-odds grow as G√N γ−1/4, the model’s as N G. Their ratio is

    So bigger models lose more (4× the model size halves c), and slowing the whole schedule helps only as γ−1/4: sixteen times the sweeps doubles c. This holds when the field is weak: a = G√N(π/γ)1/4 ≪ 1. The exact CW chain agrees with the full formula, Eq. 3, for N = 1,024–4,096 and γ = 0.00025–0.004: the chain lies above the formula in all nine cases, by 0.23–1.81% (Table 2). The law does not extend to arbitrarily slow cooling at fixed N: long sampling at the final temperature recovers equilibrium, but at N = 2,048 the zero-field mean time for a mode switch is about 5 × 107 expected sweeps at β = 1.1 (Sec. 3, App. A). On the paper’s cooling schedule, a quarter of the final cooling gives c = 0.099 and four times gives 0.147: 1.48× for sixteen times the final cooling (Sec. 4, App. A).

    Chapter 6 · β 1.10

    What changes when patterns compete?

    If the largest early overlap picks the pattern, pattern 1 wins only with a larger-than-usual overlap, which carries more of the field’s preference. Competition should then keep more of it.

    Fig. 6 live · a: Hopfield, N = 256 (paper 2,048) · b, c: Gaussian draws

    A prediction; chapter 7 measures it.

    Result 6Under a Gaussian approximation, three patterns amplify c by A3 = 1.662: predicted c = 0.120 × 1.662 = 0.199.

    WhyWhy would competing patterns help?

    The Curie–Weiss model has only two modes. The paper also tests the law on a Hopfield model that stores p random patterns; each pattern and its negative is a mode. You might expect competition to make the sampler worse at honouring the model’s preference.

    The paper predicts the opposite. The field acts on pattern 1, so c is measured only on the kept runs: those that end in pattern 1 or its negative. If the largest early overlap decides, those runs won with a larger-than-usual overlap, and a larger overlap carries a stronger sign preference; c rises by an amplification factor Ap.

    The paper approximates the early overlaps by independent Gaussians with a common variance, the field shifting only the mean of overlap 1, as in the Curie–Weiss model. It assumes the largest in size decides the pattern, and takes the Hopfield model’s own preference to be the Curie–Weiss one. Small p/N guarantees neither this equality nor independent overlaps (Sec. 4).

    Approximate the p early overlaps by independent Gaussians with a common spread, and assume the largest in magnitude decides the pattern. Sign log-odds grow with the magnitude, so, to first order in the field, the kept runs express the preference more:

    Method · β 1.20

    Where do the test runs start?

    Hopfield test runs begin from a filtered start: after a brief cooling, runs labelled (1,+), nearest to pattern 1 with a + sign, are removed; the rest are reheated at β = 0.7 and cooled again.

    Not shown: that reheated runs match fresh ones (App. B).

    SourceApp. BHopfield runs

    MethodFor G > 0 the filtered start imitates a sampler settled away from the favoured mode; reheating must undo that choice (reselection).

    WhyWhy a filtered start?

    The paper tests the competition prediction on runs that choose a mode again, not on fresh starts (Sec. 4). For G > 0 the filtered start imitates a sampler that settled away from the favoured mode; reheating at β = 0.7, where the drift reduces an imbalance, must undo that choice, and the final cooling chooses again. The removal is applied at both field signs, so paired fields ±G follow one protocol.

    For a symmetric start, flipping every spin and the sign of G would give qchain(G) + qchain(−G) = 1; the removal breaks this symmetry, and its memory could shift the sum. The measured sums are consistent with one but do not establish equivalence to fresh starts (App. B).

    The steps: after two sweeps at each of the ten schedule temperatures, every run labelled (pattern 1, +) is removed, for both signs of the field. The rest reheat for 30 sweeps at β = 0.7, below the transition, with the patterns and field unchanged; then the final cooling follows the 834-sweep schedule, which begins at the hotter β = 0.6.

    The Curie–Weiss reference cCW runs through the same removal and reheating. For Curie–Weiss, 30 sweeps at β = 0.7 shrink the slowest deviation to about 1.2 × 10−4; this does not bound the memory of the filtered Hopfield runs (App. B).

    WhyHow is c measured on the network?

    Earlier chapters produced two numbers without the Hopfield runs: cCW = 0.120, computed for the Curie–Weiss sampler through the same steps as the network runs (a brief cooling, removal of the runs labelled pattern 1 positive to leave the filtered start, reheating at β = 0.7, then the final cooling; Where the runs start), and A3 = 1.662 for three patterns. Their product, 0.120 × 1.662 = 0.199, is the prediction. The figure sets it against the measurement, at the paper’s setting and at its neighbours, and lets you multiply by the one-pattern ratio to see what it changes.

    At eight field strengths the sampler’s log-odds are plotted against the model’s; a faithful sampler would put every point on the diagonal. The slope of the line through them is c. At the strongest field, 66.9% of pattern-1 samples are positive against a model reference of 97.26%, so their logits give c = 0.70/3.57 ≈ 0.20; that reference is estimated from the mean overlap in the positive mode, so it is approximate (Sec. 4, App. B). The full figure is in the Plot drawer.

    The one-pattern ratio

    cCW comes from the Curie–Weiss chain: random update times (expected sweeps) and interaction strength 1/(N−1). Hopfield sampling uses permutation sweeps (each spin once, in a fresh random order) and 1/N. With one pattern there is no competition, yet Hopfield runs keep r = 1.063 times the Curie–Weiss log-odds at N = 2,048 (1.065, 1.126, 1.063, 1.020 at N = 512 to 4,096): 0.1274 against 0.1198, from 150,000 runs per field sign. Source: paper Sec. 5, App. B; data/sampler_clock.json.

    Chapter 7 · The test →

    2The main-setting match may come from cancelling errors.

    With one pattern there is no competition, yet the network keeps 1.063 times the Curie–Weiss log-odds at N = 2,048, and 1.020 to 1.126 times over N = 512 to 4,096. The ratio comes from the update order (each spin once per sweep in random order, against random update times) and from the interaction strength (1/N in the network, 1/(N−1) in Curie–Weiss). Multiplying the three-pattern prediction by 1.063 gives 0.212, outside the measured interval, so errors in the approximations may cancel at the main setting.

    Chapter 7 · Caveats →

    3The trend with size is unexplained.

    Measured c falls about as N−0.36; the prediction cpred falls as N−0.46 (two-point slopes, no uncertainties; Eq. 4 gives N−1/2 for a fixed ramp). The two cross near N = 2,048, where the schedule was set. Without correction the predictions miss by 7% and 6% (Limit 1). Multiplying each by its one-pattern ratio (Limit 2) does not close the gap: the corrected predictions are 0.319 at N = 1,024 and 0.153 at N = 4,096, and measured c is 18% below and 4% above them. Random patterns are correlated at order N−1/2, which shifts the β at which overlaps start to grow. At finite N the transition is spread over a β range of comparable size, the critical window, so the shifts need not become negligible relative to this window as N grows. That is an untested explanation.

    Chapter 7 · The size rows →

    4The network runs never vary the cooling rate.

    The field and the sweeps per temperature are fixed in the pattern and size comparisons, so the network runs do not measure how c depends on the cooling rate in the Hopfield model. The scaling c ∝ (N²γ)−1/4 (how c changes with the cooling rate γ; paper Eq. 4) is checked only on Curie–Weiss linear ramps, where the selection formula matches the exact CW chain within 1.81%, with the chain above it in all nine cases (paper Table 2).

    Chapter 5 · Does slower fix it? →

    5The competition prediction rests on approximations.

    The factor A3 (the three-pattern amplification factor from Chapter 6) treats the early pattern overlaps as independent Gaussians with a common variance, the field shifting only the mean of overlap 1, and assumes the largest overlap decides the label. The prediction also uses the Curie–Weiss equilibrium preference in place of the Hopfield one. A small ratio p/N guarantees neither the equal preferences nor independent overlaps. The Hopfield reference is itself estimated from the mean overlap m̄1 within the positive mode, and the 95% interval on c excludes its error and the 1% approximation allowance (a fixed margin the paper adds to the reference interval).

    Chapter 6 · Competing patterns →

    6Reheated starts are not shown to equal fresh ones.

    The measured runs begin from a filtered start: a brief cooling; removal of the runs already labelled (1,+), whose largest overlap is with stored pattern 1 and is positive, under both signs of the field; then 30 sweeps of reheating at β = 0.7. For a symmetric start, flipping every spin and the sign of the field would make qchain(G) + qchain(−G) = 1; the removal breaks that argument. Each measured paired-field sum still has an interval containing 1. That does not show reheated runs equal fresh ones. The MNIST deep-Boltzmann-machine illustration (ten seeds of 24 runs) resolves only large effects of the initial state.

    7The law is for annealed Gibbs sampling.

    The selection formula assumes a slow ramp that starts well below β = 1 and little switching after the sign is chosen; Nγ ≫ 1 (model size times cooling rate γ is large) is a sufficient condition. It does not hold for arbitrarily slow cooling at fixed N: long enough sampling at the final temperature recovers the equilibrium proportions, but at N = 2,048 the zero-field mean time for a mode switch is about 5 × 107 expected sweeps at β = 1.1. Diffusion models also change their distribution gradually, but with an exact score they reverse a given noising process and need not equilibrate at each step (Biroli et al. 2024); extending the calculation to them requires their dynamics and discrete updates. Annealed importance sampling weights the samples of annealed runs to estimate equilibrium averages (Neal 2001); a future equal-cost comparison needs valid weights for the filtered start and an assessment of estimator bias and variance. Applying c to another model needs an equilibrium reference.
